## The symptom: sitting hurts, and everything else hurts too

You sit down for a meeting and within twenty minutes you're shifting, leaning forward, adjusting. Get up and there's a dull ache deep between your legs. The same spot aches when you urinate, and there's a sting when you ejaculate. Sitting is the worst.

This is the pattern of chronic pelvic pain syndrome — CPPS, often still called chronic prostatitis — and it is one of the most common urological diagnoses in men under 50.

Here is the part that's useful to know: **the burning and aching you're feeling is real, it's measurable, and inflammation is genuinely part of it. But the inflammation is usually not where you'd expect, and treating it as if it were a simple inflammation is why so many men get disappointing results.**

## The cause: it's less about the prostate than you'd think

The word "prostatitis" implies the prostate is inflamed. In CPPS, that's rarely the whole story.

**What's actually happening in most cases: the pelvic floor muscles are tight, and the nerves have become sensitised.**

The pelvic floor is a bowl of muscles holding your pelvis together. In CPPS, these muscles — particularly the levator ani and the muscles around the anus and the urethra — develop **trigger points**: tight, tender bands that hurt when pressed and that refer pain to the perineum, testicles, tailbone or lower back.

![Male pelvic floor anatomy showing trigger points in the muscles and radiating nerve pathways](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794327865_22a5b356.png)

A widely used diagnostic finding in CPPS research is that **pressure testing of the pelvic floor reproduces the man's own pain** in a substantial proportion of cases. Studies using surface EMG to confirm trigger points find them in roughly **half to 57%** of men with CPPS.

This is why sitting hurts. Sitting loads the pelvic floor continuously. Why urination stings — the urethra passes through that same muscle group. Why ejaculation burns — the pelvic floor contracts forcefully during ejaculation, and a trigger-pointed muscle responds badly.

### So where does inflammation fit?

Three places, in descending order of how well the evidence supports them:

**1. Neuroinflammation — the best-supported.** The nerve endings in a chronically irritated pelvic floor become hypersensitive. They fire at lower thresholds and amplify signals. The published research finds **higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines** in the seminal fluid and pelvic tissues of men with CPPS compared to controls. One notable study found the cytokine **IL-8 correlated directly with reported pain intensity**.

This is the real meaning of "inflammation" in CPPS: not an infected prostate, but a sensitised nervous system with an inflammatory signature.

**2. Muscle-trigger-point inflammation — local and important.** A tight, trigger-pointed muscle is genuinely inflamed at the tissue level. This is a mechanical form of inflammation, and it's why the treatment that works best is a physical one.

**3. Prostatic inflammation — the smallest share.** The NIH classification separates inflammatory CPPS (IIIA, white cells in expressed prostate fluid) from non-inflammatory (IIIB, without). But this distinction has weaker clinical value than the muscle explanation, which is why guidelines have quietly moved away from it.

### What about infection?

This matters enough to state plainly. **Antibiotics generally do not outperform placebo for CPPS.** The AUA guideline's recommendation against routine antibiotic use reflects trials showing no meaningful benefit, because bacteria are not the cause in most cases.

"Genuine bacterial infection" — acute or chronic bacterial prostatitis — is a different disease that does need antibiotics. The distinction is real and worth holding onto: **if you have fever, chills, and difficulty passing urine, that's the bacterial presentation. If you have sitting pain that flares and fades over months, that's CPPS.**

## The result: what actually happens when you treat it this way

Here's where the evidence is most encouraging, and where the practical outcome lives.

### The best-evidenced treatment is physical, not pharmaceutical

![A male physiotherapist working with a male patient on a treatment table](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794328145_b4a335ec.png)

**Pelvic floor physical therapy** has the strongest support of any treatment for CPPS. The approach isn't generic — a physiotherapist trained in pelvic floor work specifically:

- **Assesses the pelvic floor by palpation** to find your trigger points
- **Releases them** with manual techniques and myofascial work
- **Retrains the muscles to relax** rather than guard
- **Teaches you a daily relaxation routine** — the part that determines whether it holds

The functional outcome that matters: when the trigger points calm down, the referring pain usually eases with them. Studies report meaningful reductions in pain scores, and the CPPS-specific symptom measure (NIH-CPSI) improves alongside it.

**About 50–57% of men with CPPS have confirmed pelvic floor trigger points** — which means this treatment is applicable to roughly half of all cases, and it's the one that targets the actual mechanism.

### The result for the sitting pain specifically

This is the practical answer the article exists to give:

1. **The trigger points get released.** Direct pressure work on the identified taut bands reduces the referred pain they create.
2. **The guarding pattern breaks.** Chronic pelvic floor guarding is a learned response. Once interrupted consistently, the muscles start returning to baseline tone.
3. **Sitting becomes tolerable again.** That sounds trivial. It isn't — for a man whose job involves sitting, it's the difference between managing his day and avoiding chairs.
4. **The urinary and ejaculatory symptoms often settle with it,** because they're downstream of the same muscle dysfunction.

### The honest limits

**Antibiotics:** no meaningful benefit over placebo for CPPS. Worth repeating because it's the most common unnecessary treatment, and because a failed antibiotic course often convinces men the diagnosis was wrong.

**Anti-inflammatory drugs:** they can reduce symptoms in some men, and they work differently from antibiotics — but the evidence for treating inflammation as the *primary* target in CPPS is limited. The AUA guideline's position is that evidence is **insufficient** to support treating CPPS as primarily an inflammatory condition. NSAIDs and the newer agents have a place, but they are not the main event.

**Antibiotics with anti-inflammatory cover:** for genuinely inflammatory (IIIA) CPPS, some trials show modest benefit, and response rates are better in men who haven't had repeated antibiotic courses. Still a secondary option.

**Surgery:** reserved for genuinely refractory cases, with variable results.

### The lifestyle results are real but unglamorous

Sitting breaks, a cushion rather than a hard chair, cycling adjustments if you ride, consistent sleep, and reducing alcohol. These sound trivial because they are modest — but sitting modification in particular often produces symptom change within days to weeks, which is faster than most medical therapies for this condition.

And the thing worth understanding: **these aren't separate from the treatment.** They work because they reduce mechanical loading on trigger points and reduce the sympathetic drive that keeps the muscles guarded.

## The bottom line

If sitting hurts and urination stings and ejaculation burns, the most likely explanation isn't an inflamed prostate that needs an anti-inflammatory drug. It's a pelvic floor with trigger points and a nervous system that has learned to amplify them.

That's genuinely good news, because trigger points respond to treatment — and the best-supported treatment for them is hands-on physical work plus retraining, not a prescription.

The result to aim for isn't a lower number on an inflammation test. It's being able to sit through a meeting without shifting.
