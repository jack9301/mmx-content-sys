PSA testing is the most contested routine test in men's health, and the disagreement is not a sign that somebody is confused. Three major bodies issue different recommendations, and each is working from the same trial data. Here is what each actually says, what a PSA number does and doesn't tell you, and what the test process involves.

## What PSA is, and what it isn't

PSA is a protein made by prostate cells. It exists in every man's bloodstream, and the prostate produces more of it as a normal response to ageing, benign enlargement, infection, and cancer alike.

What PSA cannot do is diagnose prostate cancer. It is a risk marker, not a detector. Many men with confirmed prostate cancer have a PSA inside the normal range, and a large share of men with a raised PSA have no cancer at all. The US Preventive Services Task Force puts it plainly: the PSA test is an imperfect screening test, and men should be informed of both the potential benefits and the harms before they decide.

This matters more than it sounds. A PSA of 4.0 ng/mL is not "a cancer result." It is a number that puts you in a group where cancer is more likely, and where a further test may be worth it.

## Why the guidelines disagree

![A balance scale weighing prostate cancer screening benefits against its harms, with a bladder and prostate on one side and a syringe, question marks and a distressed figure on the other. Soft watercolor medical illustration, peach and sage palette.](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794902692_a805a3d7.jpg)

The disagreement comes down to how each body weighs two things against each other: the modest reduction in prostate cancer mortality that screening produces, and the real cost of finding cancers that would never have caused harm.

**The USPSTF (2018)** recommends individual decision-making for men aged 55 to 69, graded C — meaning the benefits and harms are close enough that the decision belongs to you, made with a clinician. For men 70 and over, the recommendation is Grade D: do not screen. The task force estimated that for every 1,000 men screened between 55 and 69 over 11 years, one death from prostate cancer is prevented. The same analysis found that 28% of men with a positive PSA result would go on to have a biopsy that returned a false positive.

**The AUA/SUO (2023)** is more proactive. Average-risk men should start screening at 45. Men at higher risk — Black men, those with a strong family history, and BRCA2 carriers — should start at 40 to 45. Men under 40 should not be routinely screened. Screening repeats every 2 to 4 years, and the guideline sets a life expectancy threshold of 10 to 15 years, because screening a man who will not live long enough to benefit from finding cancer is a net harm.

**The EAU (2025)** offers a structured population programme rather than individual testing: a baseline PSA offered to all well-informed men aged 45 to 50 with a life expectancy of at least 10 years, then risk-stratified follow-up.

None of these is the odd one out. They weight the same evidence differently, and the honest answer is that the evidence itself supports more than one reasonable choice.

## What the trials actually found

The evidence base rests on two large randomised trials, and they do not say the same thing.

**PLCO (76,693 men, USA)** found no statistically significant reduction in prostate cancer mortality from screening over 11 years of follow-up. A major limitation: up to 90% of men in the control arm had an opportunistic PSA test anyway, so the comparison was partly screening versus informal screening.

**ERSPC (162,236 men, eight European countries)** did find a benefit. The 23-year follow-up published in 2025 found a prostate cancer mortality rate ratio of 0.87 (95% CI 0.80–0.95), a 13% relative reduction, with an absolute risk reduction of 0.22%. The number needed to invite to prevent one death fell from 628 at 16 years to 456 at 23 years, and the number needed to diagnose fell from 18 to 12.

The ERSPC result also carries a detail worth knowing. Prostate cancer diagnoses were 14% more common in the screening arm than the control arm (rate ratio 1.30), which is the signature of overdiagnosis: finding cancers that would never have surfaced. At 23 years, overdiagnosis stood at 27 per 1,000 men screened. Meanwhile, deaths from all other causes were identical between groups — 49% in each — so screening did not appear to shorten life overall.

**CAP (415,357 men, UK)** added a third data point. A single PSA invitation produced a modest 8% reduction in prostate cancer mortality at 15 years (0.69% versus 0.78%), with no significant benefit detectable at 10 years. Notably, the benefit came entirely from finding more low-grade and localised disease — screening did not increase detection of intermediate, high-grade or advanced tumours.

Put together: screening probably saves some lives. The number is small, the confidence intervals are wide, and the cost is a substantial quantity of unnecessary procedures.

## The main harms are real, and they are not all equal

![Cross-section of the urinary bladder and prostate gland with the prostatic urethra running through it, showing yellow lesions in the prostate tissue. 3D medical illustration.](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794880362_cb407419.jpg)

Overdiagnosis is the biggest one. Estimates vary, but screening detects a substantial proportion of tumours that would never cause symptoms or death in a man's lifetime — between 20% and 50% of screen-detected cancers, depending on age and PSA level. The USPSTF describes screening as producing a moderate to large increase in prostate cancer diagnoses, many of which are low-grade and indolent.

For an individual man, that can mean a lifetime of knowing he has cancer, with the follow-up testing and treatment decisions that follow.

Biopsy is the second. A prostate biopsy samples tissue through a needle, most often guided by transrectal ultrasound. It can cause bleeding, blood in semen, and significant pain in a substantial minority of men. Infection is the serious risk. Guidelines increasingly favour the transperineal approach, which carries a sepsis risk below 0.3% — lower than the 0.5% to 1.5% seen with transrectal biopsy even with antibiotic prophylaxis.

Treatment is the third and slowest. If screening finds a cancer that would never have caused harm, treating it can cost quality of life. Radical prostatectomy carries risks of urinary incontinence and erectile dysfunction; radiotherapy causes bowel and urinary irritation. The ProtecT trial, which followed men with localised disease for 15 years, found no significant difference in all-cause mortality between active monitoring, surgery and radiotherapy — but men on active monitoring reported better urinary and sexual function.



## How to read your own PSA

If you have a result, the number alone tells you very little. What matters is the context around it.

**Repeat it.** A raised PSA is often transient. Ejaculation, cycling, a digital rectal examination, a long car ride, recent instrumentation, and urinary infection can all push it up. Benign prostatic hyperplasia and prostatitis raise PSA persistently. The AUA guideline is explicit that an elevated PSA should be confirmed with a repeat test before secondary biomarker testing, imaging or biopsy is considered.

**Consider the trajectory.** A PSA that rises steadily over years is a different signal from one that sits at a stable level. This is why screening intervals of 2 to 4 years exist, and why a single measurement in isolation can mislead.

**Check the density, not just the number.** PSA density normalises PSA to prostate volume. A larger prostate produces more PSA without necessarily harbouring anything dangerous. A density above 0.15 ng/mL/cc points towards a higher likelihood of clinically significant cancer and is a common trigger for MRI before biopsy.

**Know what your age and prostate size make normal.** There is no single reference range that works for every man. This is why the EAU recommends a baseline PSA in your late forties — it establishes what normal looks like for you, so later change can be interpreted against your own history rather than a population average.

**Use the second-line tests when they fit.** Markers like PHI and 4Kscore, and the Stockholm3 test, are applied after an elevated PSA to work out whether a biopsy is needed. They report high sensitivity (around 90% to 95% for clinically significant cancer) and can add meaningfully to specificity, cutting unnecessary biopsies. They do not replace PSA, and they are not screening tests in themselves.

**If you take finasteride or dutasteride, your PSA needs adjusting.** These drugs reduce PSA by roughly half, because they shrink the prostate's PSA-producing tissue. A PSA of 3.0 on finasteride is not the same as a PSA of 3.0 without it — the upper limit of normal has to be doubled. Failing to adjust can cause real cancers to be missed.

## If your PSA is raised

The pathway after an abnormal result has become considerably less aggressive, and this is worth knowing before you walk into a clinic.

An MRI before biopsy is now standard in the AUA guideline for men who have not had a biopsy before, and it is central to EAU practice. An MRI that shows nothing suspicious can substantially reduce the need to biopsy at all. One cohort using EAU criteria found that a PSA density below 0.15 combined with no suspicious MRI finding carried a 2.4% risk of clinically significant cancer — low enough in many protocols to mean no biopsy, just continued PSA monitoring.

Where MRI does show something, a PI-RADS 3 lesion with very low suspicion can reasonably be followed rather than biopsied immediately.

A digital rectal examination has a limited role. Its positive predictive value is only about 4% to 11% when PSA is below 2.0 ng/mL, and it adds little for men with a PSA under 3. It functions as an adjunct, not a standalone test. It can also briefly raise PSA, so it is worth avoiding for roughly 48 to 72 hours before a blood test.

## Where this leaves the decision

The honest summary is that screening for prostate cancer saves some lives, at a modest rate, and the guidelines disagree about whether the arithmetic works for you personally.

For most men, the practical steps are straightforward. Know your baseline. If you are 45 or older, discuss it with a clinician who will talk you through the specific numbers for the European and UK evidence base, rather than generalising. If you are at higher risk — Black ethnicity, a strong family history, or a BRCA2 mutation — start earlier, and AUA guidance puts that at 40 to 45. If you are over 70, or your life expectancy is under 10 years, the evidence does not support screening, and the guidelines agree on that even where they disagree elsewhere.

And if a PSA comes back raised, remember what it actually is: a signal to ask more questions, not a diagnosis.
