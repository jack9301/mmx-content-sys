Sex is one of the more effective treatments for chronic pelvic pain syndrome, and the man sitting in a urologist's office with pelvic pain is usually the one who stopped having sex entirely — because it hurt.

That's backwards. The research on this is reasonably clear, and most men don't hear it.

## Why sex helps

Three mechanisms, all mechanical, none mysterious.

**The prostate contracts during ejaculation.** That contraction physically squeezes fluid from the prostate's ducts and into the urethra. This is why the prostate can feel firm and slightly tender right after ejaculation in healthy men. In a man with an inflamed or hypersensitive prostate, that same contraction is experienced as pain — and the pain resolves as the fluid clears.

**The pelvic floor contracts rhythmically during ejaculation.** These contractions move material through the urethra and contribute to the clearance of prostatic fluid. Muscles that are already guarded and tight do this less efficiently, which is part of why fluid lingers.

**Regular ejaculation prevents accumulation.** This is the clinically important part. Prostatic fluid produced continuously has to go somewhere. When it doesn't, it accumulates, and accumulation is associated with worse pain and more inflammation. Ejaculation is the clearance mechanism.

This is why both the AUA and EAU guidelines for chronic pelvic pain syndrome include regular ejaculation and sexual activity among the recommended management measures. It's not a suggestion that sex is good for you in general. It's a treatment for this specific condition.

## The post-ejaculatory pain phenomenon

This deserves its own section because it's the most common and least understood feature of CPPS.

**What it is:** a distinct pain that starts after ejaculation, usually deep in the perineum or low in the pelvis, typically lasting minutes and sometimes longer. It overlaps with, but isn't the same as, the general pelvic pain of CPPS.

**How common:** estimates vary widely across studies, which is worth stating plainly. Some report it in around a third of men with CPPS; others substantially higher. The honest framing is that a substantial minority experience it, and it's one of the more consistent features of the condition.

**The mechanism.** Several factors converge. Prostatic fluid passes through ducts that are inflamed or hypersensitive. The seminal vesicles contract and empty. The pelvic floor undergoes intense repeated contraction, and a muscle that was already guarding goes into a temporary state of deeper fatigue. And there's the inflammatory component — the "prostatitis" in the name can involve genuine inflammation, though in many men it doesn't.

**The most actionable finding: it's dose-dependent.**

This is the part that changes behavior. The relationship between ejaculation and post-ejaculatory pain isn't linear — it's a curve with a floor and a ceiling.

- **Too frequent:** fluid doesn't have time to accumulate and clear properly, and the prostate and pelvic floor are repeatedly contracted before they have recovered. Symptoms worsen.
- **Too infrequent:** fluid accumulates, the structures stay sensitised, and the next ejaculation produces a larger volume through sensitised ducts. Symptoms worsen again.
- **In between:** there's a window where the fluid clears at a sustainable rate without over-stressing the tissue.

Most men find this window by accident. A few have it backwards — thinking less sex must be better, abstaining, and then experiencing the worst flare of the whole period. **For most men with CPPS, going more often is better than going less often.** That's the opposite of the instinct, and it's why abstinence is rarely the right advice here.

## Positions, pressure, and the perineum

One anatomical fact explains a large share of the practical problems.

**The perineum** is the area between the scrotum and the anus, and it sits directly beneath the prostate. The structures that cause CPPS symptoms — the prostate, the pelvic floor, the pudendal nerve — all sit in that region, and they're sensitive to sustained compression.

**Any position that loads the perineum will aggravate symptoms.** The classic example is the position where the receiving partner is on top, because that puts the man's perineum directly against the other person's pubic bone, and that weight transfers straight into the structures underneath.

![Watercolor of a bicycle saddle with warm pressure lines over the perineal area](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1793437779_c8ad7b9b.png)

That's the same structure a bicycle saddle presses on. It's the same structure that gets compressed during long car journeys. It's the same structure that makes sitting uncomfortable in a hard chair. **Perineal pressure is a through-line across your entire pelvic health, sexual and otherwise.**

**Practical adjustments:**

- **Side-lying** distributes weight away from the perineum entirely
- **Supine** with the receiving partner's weight supported off the perineum
- **Position changes during** rather than committing to one for the whole session
- **Knee-chest or elbow positions** where the man supports his own weight rather than bearing load

**The rule that generalizes:** if a position causes symptoms during or immediately after, that's information, not bad luck. Your body is telling you which positions load the affected structures. Treat it as data.

## The medication paradox

This section is the one most likely to surprise you, and it connects to our [prostatitis and erectile dysfunction article](/en/articles/prostatitis-erectile-dysfunction/).

**Antidepressants are an evidence-based treatment for CPPS.** This sounds strange until you understand the mechanism: CPPS involves central sensitisation — the nervous system has learned to amplify normal signals into pain. Antidepressants, particularly tricyclics and SNRIs, reduce central pain sensitisation. They're not treating depression in this context; they're treating a sensitised nervous system.

**But the same drugs can cause erectile dysfunction.** SSRIs carry the highest risk, with ED affecting a meaningful share of men taking them — estimates in clinical reviews run substantially higher than the rates typically quoted in general prescribing information. The mechanism is increased serotonin reducing the nitric oxide signalling that erection depends on.

**So a man can be helped and hurt by the same prescription at the same time.** The antidepressant improves his pelvic pain. The same antidepressant affects his erectile function. If he's taking one for CPPS and has developed ED, that medication is a plausible contributor — and it's a conversation worth having rather than something to endure silently.

**PDE5 inhibitors complicate it further.** Tadalafil has documented benefit in CPPS-associated ED, and sildenafil produced sustained improvement over four years in men with CPPS and ED, including in men classified as unlikely responders. So a man with CPPS on an SSRI who also has ED has a reasonable medication option that works on the vascular and smooth muscle dysfunction.

## When sex is genuinely the wrong advice

This is where the general guidance stops, and getting it wrong causes harm.

**Acute bacterial prostatitis.** This is an active infection, and sexual activity will make it worse. This is the main exception to everything above, and it's the reason "regular ejaculation" advice always comes with the qualifier "except during acute infection."

**Immediately after a procedure.** Following a prostate biopsy or instrumentation, there's a window where sexual activity carries real risk of bleeding and infection.

**When pain is severe enough that penetration isn't viable.** This isn't a medical contraindication, it's a practical one, and it's more common than clinicians admit. Non-penetrative intimacy is legitimate and worth recommending explicitly, because men frequently assume it isn't.

**During a diagnostic workup.** If the question is whether symptoms relate to ejaculation at all, the pattern needs to be observed before it's manipulated.

## The partner conversation

This goes wrong more often than the sex does.

**What usually happens:** the man stops initiating because he's afraid of causing pain, or because he's afraid of disappointing his partner. The partner reads the withdrawal as loss of interest. Neither person says anything. The avoidance makes the pelvic floor worse, the stress makes the symptoms worse, and the distance between them grows.

**What partners usually want to know:** that this is a real condition, that it's treatable, and that the trajectory is improving. Almost no one is put off by the condition itself. What creates damage is the silence.

**The framing that works:** this is a treatable condition with a recovery timeline measured in months. That's not a reassurance — it's the actual evidence. And it makes the temporary difficulty legible as temporary rather than as a permanent change in the relationship.

## The timeline

**Weeks.** Something changes. Which intervention helps varies — but pelvic floor work, activity adjustment, and medication all produce measurable improvement within the first month in trials.

**Months.** Continued improvement is typical and not linear. Plateaus of several weeks are common and are the single most common reason people conclude nothing is working.

**The honest answer:** most men improve over three to six months, and the improvement comes from consistency rather than from any single intervention. Sex plays a part in that, but it's part of a broader set — pelvic floor work, movement, stress management, and medication when indicated.

**What doesn't work:** treating it as something to endure silently, or treating abstinence as the treatment.

## The bottom line

For most men with chronic pelvic pain syndrome, sex is a treatment, and avoiding it makes things worse. The clinically actionable parts are unglamorous: find the frequency window, adjust the positions that load your perineum, and take the medication question seriously in both directions.

The guideline position is consistent across the AUA and EAU, and it comes down to this — the structures involved need to move, the fluid needs to clear, and the pelvic floor needs regular, non-guarded use.
