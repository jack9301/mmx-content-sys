There is a particular way men talk about erectile dysfunction that treats it as a sealed compartment. Something happens down there, in a region disconnected from the rest of the body, and the sensible response is to treat that compartment specifically.

The evidence points somewhere else entirely. Erectile function is a vascular event governed by the same endothelium that lines your coronary arteries, dependent on the same blood pressure, blood sugar, body fat and sleep that govern everything else. It behaves like every other piece of physiology: it responds to the general condition of the body, and it gives you a signal when that condition is slipping.

This article takes that seriously. It looks at what happens to erections when the body is well conditioned, what happens when it is not, and which of the commonly offered explanations survive contact with randomised trials.

## What happens to erections when your body is in good condition?

An erection is a haemodynamic event. Sexual stimulation triggers nitric oxide release from penile endothelium, which relaxes smooth muscle in the corpora cavernosa, arterial inflow increases, and venous outflow is trapped. Every step depends on endothelial function — and endothelial function is one of the things physical conditioning measurably improves.

That mechanism is interesting but on its own it is just plausible reasoning. The stronger claim is that randomised controlled trials have actually measured the outcome.

The best available synthesis is a 2023 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Sexual Medicine* pooling **11 randomised controlled trials and 1,147 men**. Men assigned to aerobic exercise scored better on the erectile function domain of the International Index of Erectile Function than men assigned to nothing. The average improvement was **2.8 points on the 6–30 scale (95% CI 1.7–3.9)**.

What makes this more than a headline number is the pattern underneath it. The benefit grew as baseline function got worse: **+2.3 points for mild ED, +3.3 for moderate, +4.9 for severe.** Men with the worst starting function gained the most. And the effect held up under scrutiny — no evidence of publication bias, and removing any single trial left the pooled result between 2.2 and 3.0 points.

For calibration, the same paper lists the minimum clinically important difference on that scale as 2.4 points for mild ED, 6.0 for moderate and 8.4 for severe. So aerobic exercise delivered roughly 60% to 100% of the clinically meaningful threshold depending on where you started. The same analysis puts testosterone replacement at about **2 points**, shockwave therapy at **4**, and PDE5 inhibitors at **4 to 8**. Exercise sits between hormone replacement and a standard tablet.

A second meta-analysis from 2024, restricted to men **not taking PDE5 inhibitors**, found a pooled standardised mean difference of **0.81 for aerobic training**. A 2025 review reached similar conclusions.

One caveat belongs in plain sight. None of these trials compared exercise to medication. Every control group did no exercise. So the correct statement is that aerobic training produces a real, measurable improvement in erectile function — not that it matches a prescription.

## Is aerobic training enough, or does strength work matter too?

The honest answer is that the two exercise modalities are not interchangeable, and they rest on different bodies of evidence.

**Aerobic training** is where the erectile-function-specific trial evidence sits. The 2024 meta-analysis found it carried the strongest signal, and that pelvic floor muscle training alone did not produce a statistically significant improvement in erectile function.

**Resistance training** has the stronger evidence for body composition, metabolic health and insulin sensitivity, but the ED-specific randomised evidence is thinner — the 2023 meta-analysis deliberately excluded resistance-only regimens.

So the practical split is this. If the question is what will move an erectile function score, the answer is aerobic work. If the question is what will improve lean mass and long-term cardiovascular risk, resistance work has the better claim. Both are worth doing; they answer different questions.

One clarification, because it is easy to conflate. Pelvic floor training has good evidence for **pelvic pain symptoms**, where it outperforms relaxation-based approaches in randomised trials. That is a different condition with a different mechanism, and it should not be read across to erectile function.

## What does "weak body condition" actually mean?

"Run down" is not a diagnosis. It is a bundle of several specific and partially measurable states, each with a different relationship to erectile function, and each pointing somewhere different.

### Sleep apnoea

The most under-recognised contributor, and the best ratio of value to effort, because treating it addresses the daytime fatigue and the erectile problem at the same time.

Untreated obstructive sleep apnoea is associated with erectile dysfunction independently of age, body mass index and other comorbidities, and the association strengthens with severity. The mechanism is coherent: repeated intermittent hypoxia degrades endothelial function and reduces nitric oxide bioavailability, sleep fragmentation suppresses testosterone, and sympathetic activation works against the vascular relaxation an erection depends on.

The practical marker is simple. Snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing during sleep, and unrefreshing sleep alongside erectile difficulty is a combination worth taking seriously.

### Testosterone, and why the LH matters more than the total

The evidence on testosterone is more interesting than the usual "low T" story suggests.

In the Massachusetts Male Aging Study, after adjusting for confounders, there was **no association** between total testosterone, bioavailable testosterone or sex hormone-binding globulin and erectile dysfunction. But men with a **luteinising hormone of 8 IU/L or above had nearly three times the odds of ED compared with those below 6 IU/L (adjusted OR 2.91, 95% CI 1.55–5.48)**. Testosterone was associated with *reduced* ED risk only in men whose LH was above 6 IU/L.

That distinction matters. A low testosterone with a normal LH behaves differently from a low testosterone with an elevated LH, and the LH is the discriminating detail.

What follows is that testosterone replacement is not an erectile dysfunction treatment. The AUA guideline is explicit: monotherapy is not recommended for treating ED in men with hypogonadism and ED, because the meta-analytic effect is around **2.65 points**. It may be considered as combination therapy in men who have failed PDE5 inhibitors alone.

That is a narrower claim than most men expect. Low testosterone is worth identifying, particularly with fatigue, loss of morning erections, reduced libido and reduced muscle mass together. Replacing it is not an erectile function intervention.

### Metabolic syndrome in lean men

This finding is easy to miss and genuinely useful.

In a cohort of 928 men without metabolic syndrome at baseline followed for roughly 15 years, ED was associated with incident metabolic syndrome at an unadjusted relative risk of **1.35 (1.01–1.81)**. But that association was almost entirely carried by lean men:

- **BMI under 25: adjusted RR 2.09 (1.09–4.02)**
- **BMI 25 and over: RR 1.06 (0.76–1.50) — no association**

In men who were already overweight, erectile dysfunction told you nothing extra about metabolic risk. In lean men it more than doubled the risk of metabolic syndrome emerging. The implication is practical: if you are lean and your erections have deteriorated, the check is worth making.

### Medication

The most reversible cause on the list, and the one most often never considered.

Medication-related side effects account for **up to 25% of all erectile dysfunction cases**, and the figures are not subtle:

- **Atenolol: 28.8% of men developed ED, against 3.4% on metoprolol.** Beta-blockers are not a uniform class effect, and the choice within the class matters enormously.
- **Centrally acting antihypertensives:** ED in up to 70% on clonidine, 53% on alpha-methyldopa
- **SSRIs:** 50–70% incidence of sexual dysfunction, broader than ED alone
- **Tricyclic antidepressants:** adjusted OR 3.35 · **Benzodiazepines:** adjusted OR 2.34
- **Antipsychotics:** 48–75% some form of sexual dysfunction
- **Opioids:** 21–52% ED prevalence among chronic users
- **Thiazide diuretics and NSAIDs:** up to 2.4-fold increased risk, though current evidence on thiazides is mixed

Against that, **ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers and nebivolol are neutral or beneficial.** Several of them improve erectile function.

The point is not to stop your medication. It is that a change in erectile function shortly after starting something is a specific, investigable event rather than a general truth about your age. If your erections changed after a medication review, that is a conversation worth having with whoever prescribes it.

## Is erectile dysfunction a warning sign about your heart?

Yes, with an important qualifier about what kind of evidence supports it.

Erectile dysfunction is a recognised risk marker for cardiovascular disease. The AUA guideline advises counselling men that ED is a risk factor for underlying cardiovascular disease, and describes it as strong as smoking or family history. The mechanistic basis is that penile and coronary endothelium are the same tissue responding to the same insults, and the penile arteries are small enough to show endothelial decline early.

The strongest causal evidence comes from a 2024 Mendelian randomisation study, and the design matters. Mendelian randomisation uses genetic variants as natural experiments, so it is not confounded by the lifestyle factors that plague ordinary observational data. It found causal roles for obesity-related traits, type 2 diabetes, cigarette consumption, hypertension, coronary heart disease, sleep-related breathing problems, depression and alcohol consumption in ED risk.

That list is essentially a map of what makes erections worse. It is also a list of things that respond to changes.

The honest framing is that ED is a marker, not a diagnosis of impending cardiac events. The relationship is an association in most cohorts, with causality supported for the specific risk factors above. It is a reason to look at the whole picture — blood pressure, glucose handling, waist circumference, tobacco, sleep quality, alcohol, mood and medication — rather than a reason to panic.

One more useful detail. In the Massachusetts Male Aging Study, erectile function did not only decline. Among 401 men followed over time, **141 (35%) experienced remission**, while 107 of 323 (33%) with minimal or moderate ED at baseline progressed. Age and body mass index were associated with both progression and remission.

A third of men improve. That is a real number, and it is the strongest argument for treating the modifiable variables rather than accepting the outcome.

## What are the realistic options?

The honest treatment ladder, without the marketing.

**First line is PDE5 inhibitors.** They are the best-evidenced pharmacological option, producing roughly **7 points** on the erectile function scale, slightly more with tadalafil. Onset, duration and food interaction differ between them. Nitrates are an absolute contraindication, and controlled and post-marketing data show no increase in myocardial infarction or death compared with expected rates.

**Lifestyle is a genuine first-line intervention in its own right**, not a consolation prize for men who cannot take medication — which is how the 2.8-point figure should be read.

**Pelvic floor physical therapy** has solid evidence for pelvic pain symptoms. It does not have equivalent evidence for erectile function in men who are not already on PDE5 inhibitors.

**Testosterone** has a defined but narrow role, and only in men with genuine hypogonadism, usually as combination rather than replacement therapy.

**What does not work:** shockwave therapy remains investigational, and the Canadian Urological Association conditionally recommends against it. Stem cell therapy and platelet-rich plasma are experimental. And in the context of pelvic pain, the LEAP trial randomised 240 men across three arms — antibiotics, sham acupuncture with placebo antibiotics, and sham acupuncture with placebo medication — and neither active arm beat sham.

## What can you actually change?

Ranked by how much the evidence supports the claim.

**Aerobic exercise.** The strongest lifestyle evidence in the literature, measured in randomised trials, with a dose-response by baseline severity. No specific optimal routine has been established, which is itself a reason to start with what is sustainable.

**Sleep, and the possibility of apnoea.** Treating disordered breathing addresses erectile function and daytime energy simultaneously. This has the best return because it improves two things at once.

**Medication review.** Up to a quarter of cases, and often the fastest to resolve. Atenolol versus metoprolol is the concrete example. Several antihypertensives are neutral or beneficial for erections.

**Tobacco, alcohol, and blood pressure.** Causal for ED by Mendelian randomisation. The relationship between alcohol and erectile function is dose-dependent, so this is about quantity and pattern rather than abstinence.

**Mood and psychological state.** Depression and anxiety are causal contributors by the same Mendelian standard. If the problem appeared alongside a period of stress, depression, or a relationship difficulty, that is not a footnote to the diagnosis. It is part of the diagnosis.

**Resistance training.** Worth doing for body composition and metabolic health, with the caveat that the ED-specific randomised evidence is thinner than for aerobic work.

## Where this leaves you

Erectile dysfunction is a whole-body signal, and the useful question is not which tablet to request but what the signal is pointing at.

Some of it is straightforwardly mechanical: a medication, an artery, a hormone. Some of it is a body that has drifted out of condition and an erection that reflects that drift. Some of it is mood, sleep and stress, which are physiology too, not a separate category.

The parts worth acting on share a feature: they are measurable. IIEF-EF score, blood pressure, waist circumference, glucose handling, sleep study, testosterone with LH, medication list. What you cannot measure is harder to act on, and that is worth knowing before deciding what to change.
