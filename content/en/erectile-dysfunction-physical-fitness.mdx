Erectile function is a vascular event. It depends on the same endothelium that lines your coronary arteries, and on the same blood pressure, blood sugar, body fat and sleep that govern everything else. Which means it responds to your general condition, and it gives you a signal when that condition is slipping.

## What happens when your body is in good condition?

An erection runs on nitric oxide: stimulation triggers penile endothelium to release it, smooth muscle in the corpora cavernosa relaxes, blood flows in and venous outflow is trapped. Every step depends on endothelial function — one of the things physical conditioning measurably improves. Randomised trials have measured the outcome. The best available synthesis is a 2023 meta-analysis in the *Journal of Sexual Medicine* pooling **11 randomised controlled trials and 1,147 men**. Men assigned to aerobic exercise scored better on the erectile function domain of the International Index of Erectile Function than men assigned to nothing: **2.8 points on the 6–30 scale (95% CI 1.7–3.9)**.

The benefit grew as baseline function got worse — **+2.3 points for mild ED, +3.3 for moderate, +4.9 for severe**. Removing any single trial left the pooled result between 2.2 and 3.0, with no evidence of publication bias.

For calibration, the same paper lists the minimum clinically important difference as 2.4 points for mild ED, 6.0 for moderate and 8.4 for severe — so aerobic exercise delivered 60% to 100% of the clinically meaningful threshold. The same analysis puts testosterone replacement at about **2 points**, shockwave therapy at **4**, and PDE5 inhibitors at **4 to 8**.

One caveat belongs in plain sight: no trial has compared exercise to medication, because every control group did no exercise. The correct statement is that aerobic training produces a real, measurable improvement — not that it matches a prescription.

## What does "weak body condition" actually mean?

"Run down" is not a diagnosis. It is a bundle of several specific and partially measurable states, each pointing somewhere different.

### Sleep apnoea

The best ratio of value to effort, because treating it addresses the daytime fatigue and the erectile problem at the same time. Untreated obstructive sleep apnoea is associated with erectile dysfunction independently of age, body mass index and other comorbidities, and the association strengthens with severity: repeated intermittent hypoxia degrades endothelial function, sleep fragmentation suppresses testosterone, and sympathetic activation works against the vascular relaxation an erection depends on.

Snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, and unrefreshing sleep alongside erectile difficulty is worth taking seriously.

### Testosterone, and why the LH matters more than the total

In the Massachusetts Male Aging Study, after adjusting for confounders, there was **no association** between total testosterone, bioavailable testosterone or sex hormone-binding globulin and erectile dysfunction. But men with a **luteinising hormone of 8 IU/L or above had nearly three times the odds of ED compared with those below 6 IU/L (adjusted OR 2.91, 95% CI 1.55–5.48)**. Testosterone was associated with *reduced* ED risk only in men whose LH was above 6 IU/L.

That distinction matters: a low testosterone with a normal LH behaves differently from a low testosterone with an elevated LH, and the LH is the discriminating detail.

Testosterone replacement is therefore not an erectile dysfunction treatment. The AUA guideline is explicit: monotherapy is not recommended for treating ED in men with hypogonadism and ED, because the meta-analytic effect is around **2.65 points**. It may be considered as combination therapy in men who have failed PDE5 inhibitors alone.

Low testosterone is still worth identifying, particularly alongside fatigue, loss of morning erections and reduced libido. Replacing it is not an erectile function intervention.

### Metabolic syndrome in lean men

In a cohort of 928 men followed roughly 15 years, ED was associated with incident metabolic syndrome at an unadjusted relative risk of **1.35 (1.01–1.81)** — an association almost entirely carried by lean men:

- **BMI under 25: adjusted RR 2.09 (1.09–4.02)**
- **BMI 25 and over: RR 1.06 (0.76–1.50) — no association**

In men already overweight, erectile dysfunction told you nothing extra about metabolic risk. In lean men it more than doubled the risk. If you are lean and your erections have deteriorated, the check is worth making.

### Medication

The most reversible cause on the list, and the one most often never considered. Medication-related side effects account for **up to 25% of all erectile dysfunction cases**:

- **Atenolol: 28.8% of men developed ED, against 3.4% on metoprolol.** Beta-blockers are not a uniform class effect.
- **SSRIs:** 50–70% incidence of sexual dysfunction, broader than ED alone
- **Antidepressants and sedatives:** tricyclics OR 3.35, benzodiazepines OR 2.34
- **Antipsychotics:** 48–75% some form of sexual dysfunction
- **Opioids:** 21–52% ED prevalence among chronic users
- **Clonidine and alpha-methyldopa:** up to 70% and 53%
- **Thiazide diuretics and NSAIDs:** up to 2.4-fold increased risk

Against that, **ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers and nebivolol are neutral or beneficial.** Several improve erectile function.

The point is not to stop your medication. It is that a change in erectile function shortly after starting something is a specific, investigable event rather than a general truth about your age.

## Is erectile dysfunction a warning sign about your heart?

Yes, with a qualifier about what kind of evidence supports it.

The AUA guideline advises counselling men that ED is a risk factor for underlying cardiovascular disease, and describes it as strong as smoking or family history. Penile and coronary endothelium are the same tissue responding to the same insults, and the penile arteries are small enough to show endothelial decline early.

The strongest causal evidence comes from a 2024 Mendelian randomisation study. The design matters: it uses genetic variants as natural experiments, so it is not confounded by the lifestyle factors that plague ordinary observational data. It found causal roles for obesity-related traits, type 2 diabetes, smoking, hypertension, coronary heart disease, sleep-related breathing problems, depression and alcohol in ED risk.

That list is a map of what makes erections worse. It is also a list of things that respond to changes.

The honest framing is that ED is a marker, not a diagnosis of impending cardiac events. It is a reason to look at the whole picture rather than a reason to panic.

Erectile function did not only decline. Among 401 men followed over time, **141 (35%) experienced remission**, while 107 of 323 (33%) with minimal or moderate ED at baseline progressed. A third of men improve, which is the strongest argument for treating the modifiable variables rather than accepting the outcome.

## What are the realistic options?

**First line is PDE5 inhibitors.** The best-evidenced pharmacological option, producing roughly **7 points** on the erectile function scale, slightly more with tadalafil. Nitrates are an absolute contraindication, and controlled and post-marketing data show no increase in myocardial infarction or death compared with expected rates.

**Lifestyle is a genuine first-line intervention in its own right**, not a consolation prize for men who cannot take medication — which is how the 2.8-point figure should be read.

**Pelvic floor physical therapy** has solid evidence for pelvic pain symptoms, where it outperforms relaxation-based approaches. It has no equivalent evidence for erectile function in men not already on PDE5 inhibitors — a different condition, and the two should not be read across.

**Testosterone** has a narrow role, and only in men with genuine hypogonadism, usually as combination rather than replacement therapy.

**What does not work:** shockwave therapy remains investigational, and the Canadian Urological Association conditionally recommends against it. Stem cell therapy and platelet-rich plasma are experimental.

## What can you actually change?

Ranked by how much the evidence supports the claim.

**Aerobic exercise.** The strongest lifestyle evidence in the literature, with a dose-response by baseline severity. No optimal routine has been established, which is itself a reason to start with what is sustainable.

**Sleep, and the possibility of apnoea.** Treating disordered breathing addresses erectile function and daytime energy at once. The best return, because it improves two things simultaneously.

**Medication review.** Up to a quarter of cases, and often the fastest to resolve. Atenolol versus metoprolol is the concrete example.

**Tobacco, alcohol, and blood pressure.** Causal for ED by Mendelian randomisation. The alcohol relationship is dose-dependent, so this is about quantity and pattern rather than abstinence.

**Mood and psychological state.** Depression and anxiety are causal contributors by the same Mendelian standard. If the problem appeared alongside stress, depression, or a relationship difficulty, that is not a footnote to the diagnosis. It is part of the diagnosis.

**Resistance training.** Worth doing for body composition and metabolic health, though the ED-specific randomised evidence is thinner than for aerobic work.

## Where this leaves you

The useful question is not which tablet to request but what the signal is pointing at. Some of it is mechanical: a medication, an artery, a hormone. Some of it is a body that has drifted out of condition. Some of it is mood, sleep and stress, which are physiology too, not a separate category.

The parts worth acting on share a feature: they are measurable. IIEF-EF score, blood pressure, waist circumference, glucose handling, sleep study, testosterone with LH, medication list. What you cannot measure is harder to act on.
