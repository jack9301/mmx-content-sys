## The short answer

Interstitial cystitis (also called bladder pain syndrome) and chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS) share most of their symptoms — pain, urinary urgency, frequency, perineal pressure. They are different conditions with different root causes, different diagnostic paths, and different treatments. They are also frequently misdiagnosed for each other. A man who has been told he has prostatitis for years, and is not improving on prostate-focused treatment, may actually have IC. A woman told she has IC, with no bladder findings on cystoscopy, may actually have pelvic floor dysfunction.

This article walks through what the two conditions actually are, how to tell them apart, and what to do if you are not sure which one you have.

## Key Takeaways

- **They share most symptoms, but the root cause is different.** IC is a bladder lining condition. CPPS is a pelvic floor and nervous system condition.
- **Misdiagnosis between the two is common.** Estimates suggest 30-50% of pelvic pain diagnoses get revised within the first 2 years.
- **The diagnostic test that actually matters differs.** Cystoscopy with hydrodistension is the gold standard for IC. Pelvic floor exam is the gold standard for CPPS.
- **Treatment differs in important ways.** Pentosan and bladder instillations work for IC, not for CPPS. Alpha-blockers and pelvic floor physical therapy work for CPPS, less so for IC.
- **Pelvic floor PT helps both — but for different reasons.** In IC, the muscles protect a painful bladder. In CPPS, the muscles are the problem itself.

## What IC actually is

Interstitial cystitis is a chronic pain condition of the bladder wall. The defining feature is damage or dysfunction of the bladder's protective lining — the glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer. When that layer is compromised, urine contents (which include potassium and other irritants) seep into the bladder wall itself. The result is pain, urgency, frequency, and a distinctive sensation of bladder pressure that is often described as "razor blades" or "acid."

IC is more common in women than in men, but the male prevalence is higher than most clinicians used to believe. Population studies suggest roughly 1-3% of men meet diagnostic criteria, though many are undiagnosed or misdiagnosed.

The key clinical features of IC:

- **Pain that worsens as the bladder fills** and improves after voiding
- **Urinary urgency and frequency out of proportion to urine volume** — feeling like you need to go when you have just gone
- **Pain above the pubic bone**, in the lower abdomen, or in the urethra
- **Symptoms that flare with certain foods and drinks** — coffee, citrus, alcohol, spicy food
- **No bacteria on standard urine culture** (or only very transient findings)

IC can occur with or without visible Hunner's lesions on cystoscopy. The presence of Hunner's lesions (reddened, inflamed patches on the bladder wall) indicates a more severe subtype called Hunner-type IC; their absence points to non-Hunner IC. Both are real and treatable.

## What CPPS actually is

Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS, NIH Category III prostatitis) is a chronic pain condition centered on the pelvic floor muscles, nerves, and connective tissue. The prostate itself is involved in some cases, but it is rarely the primary driver. The defining feature is a pelvic floor that is tight, tender, and in a heightened state of guarding — sometimes with active trigger points.

CPPS is more common in men than in women. Population estimates suggest 5-10% of men experience CPPS symptoms at some point in their lives. It is the most common urology diagnosis in men under 50.

The key clinical features of CPPS:

- **Perineal pain** — often described as sitting on a golf ball, or pressure between the scrotum and rectum
- **Pain during or after ejaculation** — burning, aching, or cramping for hours
- **Urinary frequency and urgency** that mimics a UTI but with negative cultures
- **Pelvic floor tenderness on physical exam** — reproducible with digital rectal exam of the levator ani
- **Symptoms that worsen with stress, sitting, and prolonged sexual activity**

CPPS is closely related to (and overlaps with) chronic pelvic floor dysfunction, CPFD. Many clinicians use the terms interchangeably. The mechanism is the same: a guarded, hypertonic pelvic floor.

## How to tell them apart

The two conditions are easy to confuse because the symptoms overlap heavily. The table below lays out the differences as they actually present in clinic.

| Feature | Interstitial cystitis | CPPS |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary pain location** | Above pubic bone, bladder, urethra | Perineum, pelvic floor, sometimes testicles or rectum |
| **Pain when bladder fills** | Yes — worse as bladder fills | Sometimes, but less consistent |
| **Pain relief after voiding** | Yes, often | Sometimes, but less reliable |
| **Pain during ejaculation** | Less common | Common |
| **Food and drink triggers** | Common — coffee, citrus, alcohol | Less consistent |
| **Pelvic floor tenderness on exam** | Secondary (muscles guarding the painful bladder) | Primary — usually reproducible trigger points |
| **Cystoscopy findings** | Hunner's lesions, glomerulations, or visible inflammation | Usually normal |
| **Standard urine culture** | Negative or minimal growth | Negative |
| **Response to prostate massage** | Often worse or unchanged | Variable — sometimes helps, sometimes worsens |
| **Response to pentosan (Elmiron)** | Often meaningful | Usually unhelpful |

The two questions that most reliably separate IC from CPPS are:

1. **Does pain worsen as your bladder fills and improve after you void?** If yes, IC is more likely.
2. **Do you have reproducible pelvic floor tenderness on physical exam?** If yes, CPPS is more likely.

Neither of these is 100% sensitive or specific. Both conditions can produce features of the other. A clinician treating either condition should evaluate for both.

## How misdiagnosis happens

The misdiagnosis rate between IC and CPPS is high — likely 30-50%, depending on the population. The reasons are practical:

- **Standard urology training leans toward "prostatitis"** for any male pelvic pain. Many urologists default to this diagnosis without a full workup.
- **IC has historically been underdiagnosed in men.** The condition was thought to be rare in males for decades. That bias persists in some clinical settings.
- **The diagnostic test for IC (cystoscopy with hydrodistension)** is invasive, requires anesthesia, and is not always performed.
- **The diagnostic test for CPPS (pelvic floor exam)** requires a trained examiner. Many urologists do not routinely perform focused pelvic floor assessment.

The practical result: many men spend years on treatments that don't work — repeated courses of antibiotics, prostate-focused therapy, prostate massage, alpha-blockers — because the underlying condition was never correctly diagnosed.

A man with refractory CPPS who has tried multiple prostate-focused therapies without lasting improvement should ask about IC. The same is true for a patient with refractory IC who hasn't had pelvic floor muscle assessment.

## What the diagnostic workup should include

A proper workup for chronic pelvic pain in a man includes several components. None of these are optional in a thorough evaluation.

**History.** Where exactly is the pain? When does it occur? What makes it better or worse? Does it relate to bladder filling, voiding, ejaculation, sitting, stress? Are there food or drink triggers? How long has it been present? Has it been progressive?

**Physical exam.** Includes abdominal exam, genital exam, digital rectal exam with focused pelvic floor assessment. The clinician should palpate the levator ani, obturator internus, pubococcygeus, and external anal sphincter for tenderness, trigger points, and resting tone. A normal prostate on DRE does not exclude CPPS.

**Urinalysis and culture.** Standard, including post-prostatic massage urine if prostatitis is suspected. A positive culture points to acute or chronic bacterial prostatitis, not IC or CPPS.

**Cystoscopy with hydrodistension.** The gold standard for IC diagnosis. Performed under anesthesia. The bladder is filled and inspected for Hunner's lesions, glomerulations (small punctate hemorrhages after distension), and overall appearance. Normal cystoscopy does not exclude non-Hunner IC but does exclude Hunner-type IC.

**Urodynamics.** Useful when the diagnosis is unclear or when neurological causes are considered. Most men with IC or CPPS have normal or near-normal urodynamics, but the test rules out other conditions.

**MRI.** Not routine but useful if there is concern for structural abnormalities, abscess, or other pelvic pathology.

The workup is incomplete without cystoscopy. A patient who has been told they have prostatitis for years without cystoscopy has not had a full evaluation.

## How treatment differs

The treatments that work for IC and CPPS are different. This is where misdiagnosis costs the most — months or years of ineffective treatment.

**Treatments that work for IC but not CPPS:**

- **Pentosan polysulfate (Elmiron).** The only FDA-approved oral drug for IC. Coats the bladder lining, restoring the GAG layer. Works for some patients over months.
- **Bladder instillations (DMSO, heparin, lidocaine).** Delivered directly into the bladder via catheter. Reduce bladder inflammation and pain.
- **Diet modification.** Avoiding bladder irritants (coffee, citrus, alcohol, spicy food, artificial sweeteners). One of the most reliable non-pharmacological interventions for IC.
- **Cystoscopy with hydrodistension itself can be therapeutic.** The procedure stretches the bladder wall and can relieve symptoms for weeks or months in some patients.

**Treatments that work for CPPS but not IC:**

- **Pelvic floor physical therapy.** The first-line treatment for CPPS. Targeted internal and external work to release trigger points and lower resting tone. Takes weeks to months.
- **Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin).** Relax smooth muscle in the bladder neck and prostate. Helpful in some CPPS patients, particularly those with urinary symptoms.
- **5-alpha-reductase inhibitors.** Useful if BPH coexists with CPPS, less so for pure CPPS.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline).** Central pain modulation. Helpful for some CPPS patients.
- **Anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).** Modest short-term benefit in CPPS.

**Treatments that can help both:**

- **Pelvic floor physical therapy.** Helpful in both, but for different reasons. In IC, releasing pelvic floor tension reduces guarding of a painful bladder. In CPPS, releasing pelvic floor tension treats the primary problem.
- **Stress management.** Stress worsens both conditions, partly through pelvic floor tension. Effective for both, though more central to CPPS.
- **Avoiding known triggers.** Different triggers in each condition, but the principle is the same.

The treatment mismatch is the cost of misdiagnosis. A man with IC treated for CPPS with pelvic floor PT alone will see some improvement but not the meaningful relief pentosan or instillations could provide. A man with CPPS treated for IC with bladder instillations is unlikely to see much benefit at all.

## What to do if you are not sure

Many patients end up with an unclear diagnosis. The standard approach is to pursue both workups in parallel.

**Step 1.** Make sure basic workup is complete: history, focused pelvic floor exam, urinalysis, post-prostatic massage urine if indicated.

**Step 2.** If IC is on the differential, get cystoscopy. This often resolves the question definitively.

**Step 3.** If pelvic floor findings are prominent, treat as CPPS initially with pelvic floor PT for 8-12 weeks. Reassess.

**Step 4.** If bladder symptoms dominate, especially with food triggers and pain relieved by voiding, treat as IC with pentosan or instillations. Reassess.

**Step 5.** If both workups are unrevealing or both treatments are partially effective, a chronic overlapping pain syndrome is likely. Treat the most bothersome symptoms with the most relevant interventions.

The honest summary is that some patients never get a clean diagnosis. They have chronic pelvic pain that responds partially to multiple approaches. This is more common than the textbook suggests. Treatment should focus on symptom control and quality of life, not on forcing a single label.

## The relationship between the two conditions

One last point worth noting: IC and CPPS can occur together. A man with long-standing CPPS who develops bladder filling pain may have developed a secondary IC component. A woman with long-standing IC can develop pelvic floor dysfunction in response to the pain. Treating both conditions simultaneously, when both are present, often produces better results than treating one at a time.

The clinical reality is that chronic pelvic pain is rarely a single, clean diagnosis. It is usually a constellation of overlapping mechanisms — bladder, pelvic floor, nervous system, central sensitization, sometimes the prostate itself. The labels are useful for matching treatment to mechanism, but they are not exclusive.

## Getting the right evaluation

If you have chronic pelvic pain and have not had cystoscopy, ask about it. If you have not had a focused pelvic floor exam, ask for one. If you have been told you have prostatitis without these evaluations, the diagnosis may not yet be fully established.

The misdiagnosis rate is high, but it is correctable. Most patients who get a clear, accurate diagnosis of either IC or CPPS — and treatment matched to that diagnosis — see meaningful improvement. The ones who don't improve are usually the ones whose workup was incomplete or whose treatment was not matched to their actual condition.

The right evaluation is the foundation. The right treatment follows from it.
