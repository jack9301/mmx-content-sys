## The short answer

Kegels strengthen the pelvic floor. That is what they do. They are the right exercise when a weak pelvic floor is the problem, and the wrong exercise when a tight pelvic floor is the problem. Most men with chronic pelvic pain, prostatitis, or chronic pelvic pain syndrome have pelvic floors that are already too tight. For those men, Kegels often make symptoms worse, not better. The exercise that helps them is the reverse — pelvic floor drops, sometimes called reverse Kegels.

This article walks through when Kegels help, when they hurt, and how to tell which one you need.

## Key Takeaways

- **Kegels are strength exercises.** They strengthen the pelvic floor. Useful for some goals, harmful for others.
- **CPPS and pelvic pain patients usually have pelvic floors that are too tight.** Kegels make them worse.
- **Reverse Kegels (pelvic floor drops) are the right exercise for most men with prostate or pelvic pain.** They release, rather than strengthen.
- **Post-prostatectomy incontinence is the one clear case where Kegels are evidence-based.** Men recovering from prostate surgery benefit from a structured Kegel program.
- **The default answer for men with prostate symptoms is assessment before exercise.** A trained pelvic floor physical therapist can tell you which you need.

## What a Kegel actually is

A Kegel is a contraction of the pelvic floor muscles — the levator ani, pubococcygeus, bulbospongiosus, and related muscles. The simplest way to think about it: it is the muscle action you would use to stop the flow of urine midstream, or to stop yourself from passing gas.

Kegels were originally developed by Dr. Arnold Kegel in 1948 for postpartum urinary incontinence in women. They worked, and the technique has been applied broadly since. For pelvic floor strengthening — when weakness is the issue — Kegels are evidence-based and effective.

The complication is that pelvic floor problems are not all the same.

## Why Kegels can hurt

A pelvic floor that is too tight is the issue underlying most chronic pelvic pain conditions — CPPS, chronic prostatitis, persistent perineal pain, painful ejaculation, urinary urgency that mimics UTI but with negative cultures. In these cases, the pelvic floor is in a state of chronic guarding. Resting tone is elevated. Trigger points are common. The muscle has adapted to a high-tension state.

Strengthening an already-tight muscle does not help. It makes the problem worse. The pelvic floor becomes tighter still. Trigger points become more irritated. Resting tone climbs higher.

This is the contradiction at the heart of online prostate health advice. "Do your Kegels" is almost universal advice for men with prostate or urinary symptoms. For men with CPPS, that advice is often wrong.

A real pattern from clinical practice:

A man develops perineal pain after a stressful period. He is told by a primary care provider to do Kegels "for prostate health." He does them daily for several weeks. The pain gets worse. The urinary urgency gets worse. He sees a urologist, who diagnoses chronic prostatitis and prescribes antibiotics. They do not help. He sees another urologist, who diagnoses CPPS and recommends pelvic floor physical therapy. The pelvic floor physical therapist finds trigger points and high resting tone — and explains that Kegels have been part of the problem.

This is not a rare scenario. It is a common one.

## When Kegels actually help

Kegels are evidence-based and beneficial in specific clinical situations. The clearest one is post-prostatectomy incontinence. Men who have had prostate surgery often develop stress urinary incontinence. Pelvic floor strengthening — supervised, progressive, ideally with a trained physical therapist — helps. The Cochrane review on this topic concludes that preoperative and postoperative pelvic floor muscle training improves continence recovery after radical prostatectomy.

Outside of post-prostatectomy incontinence, Kegels are useful in any situation where pelvic floor weakness is the issue. That includes:

- **Long-term urinary incontinence unrelated to surgery**
- **Fecal incontinence with weak sphincter tone**
- **Sexual function issues where weak pelvic floor contributes to erectile or ejaculatory problems**

In all these cases, the clinical pattern is weakness, not tightness. The pelvic floor is under-recruited. The right intervention is strengthening.

The simpler diagnostic question is: **is your pelvic floor weak or tight?** Strengthening helps one. Releasing helps the other. Picking the wrong one is a common mistake, and a common cause of treatment failure.

## How to tell if your pelvic floor is tight or weak

A trained pelvic floor physical therapist can tell you definitively. They assess resting tone, trigger points, ability to contract and relax, and the relationship between the pelvic floor and your symptoms. This is the gold standard.

Without a clinician, there are some rough self-assessments that can guide you, though they are not substitutes for examination.

**Signs your pelvic floor is tight (not weak):**

- Painful ejaculation or post-ejaculation aching
- Perineal pain or pressure that worsens with sitting
- Urinary urgency with small urine volume
- A sense of incomplete emptying
- Constipation or difficulty initiating a bowel stream
- Pain during or after sex that doesn't fit a clear pattern

**Signs your pelvic floor is weak (not tight):**

- Stress incontinence — leaking with coughing, sneezing, lifting
- Post-void dribbling unrelated to obstruction
- Fecal incontinence or difficulty holding gas
- Genital or perineal numbness
- Erectile dysfunction with poor rigidity

These two patterns often coexist. A man can have elements of both — high resting tone in some muscles, low tone in others. A clinician can distinguish them. Self-assessment can give you a hint but should not be the basis for a treatment plan.

If most of your symptoms are in the first list, the answer is almost certainly not Kegels. It is the reverse.

## What a reverse Kegel actually is

A reverse Kegel — sometimes called a pelvic floor drop or pelvic floor lengthening — is the opposite of a Kegel. Where a Kegel contracts the pelvic floor, a reverse Kegel releases and lengthens it.

The simplest cue: bear down gently as if you are starting to urinate, or as if you are passing gas. The motion is down and out, not up and in. The pelvic floor descends, the muscles lengthen, and resting tone drops.

A reverse Kegel feels very different from a Kegel. There is no muscle contraction you can feel. The sensation is one of letting go. New practitioners sometimes describe it as "nothing is happening" — which is a sign you are doing it right.

A typical progression:

**Week 1.** Reverse Kegels while lying down, in a quiet place. Five repetitions, three times per day. Focus on letting the pelvic floor descend gently. Breathe.

**Week 2.** Add reverse Kegels while seated, particularly if sitting is a symptom trigger. Note any change in urinary urgency or perineal pressure.

**Week 3.** Combine reverse Kegels with breathwork — long exhale through the mouth, reverse Kegel on the exhale, hold the release for a count of 5.

**Week 4.** Use reverse Kegels preemptively — before stressful events, before sexual activity if ejaculation pain is a concern, before long meetings if sitting is uncomfortable.

The full effect takes weeks to months. This is one of the rare interventions that does not produce immediate benefit, even if the patient is doing it correctly.

## The standard mistake

The standard mistake, repeated across thousands of patient histories, is this:

A man has urinary or pelvic symptoms. He is told — by a clinician, a forum, a YouTube video — to do Kegels. He does them diligently for several weeks. Symptoms worsen or do not improve. He concludes that "exercises don't work for me." He abandons pelvic floor work entirely.

The actual conclusion should have been: Kegels weren't the right exercise. The pelvic floor was already tight. Strengthening it made things worse. A different intervention — pelvic floor physical therapy, reverse Kegels, or both — was needed.

This pattern is so common that some pelvic floor physical therapists now explicitly ask new patients whether they have been doing Kegels. If yes, and if their symptoms fit the tight pattern, the first intervention is often to stop doing Kegels and let the pelvic floor relax.

## What to do instead

If your pelvic floor is tight — which is the most common pattern in men with CPPS, chronic prostatitis, or persistent perineal pain — the interventions that work include:

- **Pelvic floor physical therapy.** The first-line treatment for CPPS. A trained therapist does internal and external work to release trigger points and lower resting tone. Eight to twelve weekly sessions is a typical course.
- **Reverse Kegels (pelvic floor drops).** The home exercise component. Can be done daily.
- **Breathwork and down-regulation.** Slow breathing with extended exhale activates the parasympathetic nervous system. The pelvic floor follows. Useful both as an acute relaxation technique and a long-term practice.
- **Avoiding pelvic floor overload.** Long sitting, aggressive edging, marathon masturbation sessions, and high-stress states all load the pelvic floor. Reducing these reduces symptoms.
- **Warm baths or perineal heat.** Heat relaxes muscle. Useful before pelvic floor work or before bed.

None of these are substitutes for pelvic floor PT in moderate-to-severe CPPS, but they support recovery and reduce symptoms day-to-day.

## When assessment matters

If you are not sure whether your pelvic floor is tight or weak, the right next step is assessment by a trained pelvic floor physical therapist. This is not a luxury or an optional extra. It is the diagnostic step that determines whether Kegels help, reverse Kegels help, or both.

Finding a pelvic floor PT:

- Many major cities have at least one. Search "pelvic floor physical therapy near me" or check the Herman & Wallace practitioner directory.
- Telehealth consultations are increasingly available for initial assessments.
- The first session typically involves history, external exam, and discussion. Internal assessment follows if appropriate.

A typical first session is 45-60 minutes and provides a clear answer about whether your pelvic floor is tight, weak, or a mix.

## The exercise that almost always helps

For men with chronic pelvic pain, the exercise that reliably helps is not Kegels — it is the combination of reverse Kegels plus pelvic floor PT. Most men see meaningful improvement over weeks to months when this is done correctly.

The exercise that almost always makes things worse is Kegels in a patient who already has a tight pelvic floor.

The exercise that helps post-prostatectomy is Kegels, supervised, progressive, ideally with biofeedback or a trained PT.

Three different recommendations for three different clinical scenarios. The right one depends on what is actually going on in your pelvic floor.

If you don't know which you need, the answer is to find out — not to assume Kegels are universally correct.
