## The symptoms: what you're actually seeing

You glance down and the water is pink. Or brown, like weak tea. Or faintly rust-coloured, and you spend a while wondering whether you ate beetroot yesterday.

The name for this is **haematuria**. If you can see it with the naked eye it is **gross haematuria**. If it takes a microscope to find it, it is **microscopic haematuria**.

Those two get treated as one topic almost everywhere, and that is a mistake. The numbers are not close.

**Visible blood in urine carries a 30–40% chance of a urinary tract cancer.** Blood found incidentally on a routine urinalysis carries about 3%. In a young man with no risk factors, well under 1%.

So before anything else, work out which of those two you are dealing with. If you can see it, keep reading. If a form told you about it, the second half of this article is the one for you.

### The colour is a clue about where it came from

![The colour range of urine, from light yellow through amber and brown to red](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794730609_ed20a891.jpg)

Blood in urine is not a diagnosis. It is a symptom, and it came from somewhere in the urinary tract. The colour narrows down where.

| What you see | Where it usually came from |
|---|---|
| Bright red, pink | Bladder, prostate, urethra |
| Tea, cola, brown | Kidney, ureter |
| Rust or smoky | Either, or old blood. Also food and some drugs |
| Pink only on the last few drops | Prostate or urethra |

Two caveats, because people get caught out by both.

Food and medication change urine colour with no blood involved at all. Beetroot, blackberries and rhubarb do it reliably, as do some vitamin supplements. And colour is not a guarantee in the other direction either. Bladder cancer can bleed bright red, and a kidney stone can produce surprisingly pale urine.

Useful clue, not a diagnosis. Which is exactly why what happens next matters more than what the colour suggests.

### The other symptoms that come with it

What the blood is accompanied by usually narrows it down more than the colour does.

**Burning when you pass urine, going more often, urgency, and pain low in the abdomen** points towards infection.

**Severe colicky pain in the side or groin, coming in waves** points towards a stone. One caveat: a stone that has worked its way into the bladder can bleed noticeably with little or no pain.

**Poor urinary flow, a weak stream, difficulty emptying** points towards the prostate — BPH or prostatitis.

**Painful ejaculation, perineal or pelvic discomfort, sitting pain** points towards prostatitis or CPPS.

**Swollen ankles, protein in the urine, high blood pressure** points towards kidney disease.

**Nothing at all** — no pain, no urinary symptoms, the blood just appeared — is the presentation that needs the most respect, because bladder cancer often has no other symptoms.

## The causes

### Urinary tract infection

By a wide margin the most common cause, and the easiest to deal with. It comes with burning when you pass urine, frequency, urgency, and usually pain low in the abdomen.

![Kidney cross-section showing stones in the calyces, renal pelvis and upper ureter](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794730619_9d2effd7.jpg)

### Kidney stones

Stones cause bleeding in about 20–25% of cases. Roughly one in five urology referrals for blood in urine turns out to be a stone. The blood is usually visible, and classically comes with severe colicky pain in the loin or groin that comes in waves.

A stone that has moved into the bladder can cause visible blood with little or no pain at all.

### An enlarged prostate

Common over 50. The enlarged gland can stretch the veins in the bladder neck, producing visible blood. This is why BPH is on the AUA's list of causes of microscopic haematuria.

### Prostatitis and chronic pelvic pain syndrome

Your area of interest, and relevant here. Inflammation in and around the prostate can cause blood in urine, and in acute bacterial prostatitis it frequently does. It typically comes with urination pain, frequency, painful ejaculation, and pelvic or perineal discomfort.

### Bladder or kidney cancer

Uncommon overall, but it is the reason visible blood matters so much. Roughly 30–40% of visible haematuria turns out to be a urinary tract cancer — a figure that holds across the American College of Physicians, the American College of Radiology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network.

The DETECT study, which the AUA guideline leans on, found **13.2%** of patients referred with visible haematuria had a urinary tract cancer, against **3.1%** of those referred with microscopic haematuria.

![Three-dimensional cross-section of the male bladder and prostate gland](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794730604_9fe4c467.jpg)

### Kidney disease

Problems with the glomeruli, the kidney's filtering units, produce tea or cola-coloured urine, often alongside protein in the urine, swelling in the legs, or high blood pressure. The AUA lists this as a cause of microscopic haematuria.

### A blood thinner

More on this below, because it is the most commonly misread.

### Exercise

Strenuous exercise, particularly running, can cause temporary blood in urine. It typically resolves within about 48 hours. But it needs to be *confirmed* by a clean repeat test, not assumed. Bladder cancer also causes intermittent bleeding, and "I was running last week" is exactly the story that delays a diagnosis.

### Four mistakes that delay diagnoses

**"I'm on warfarin, so it's the blood thinner."**

This is the one that worries me most. A systematic review found that among patients on anticoagulants who presented with haematuria, **24% had a urinary tract malignancy**. A blood thinner does not create the bleeding. It stops the clot that would otherwise seal it, so an existing source becomes visible. The workup is the same either way.

**"My prostate is enlarged, that's obviously it."**

BPH is a reasonable explanation and it is on the guideline list. It still cannot be assumed. The AUA is explicit that gross haematuria in a man with BPH has to be *proven* prostatic in origin, because the alternative is the one that matters.

**"It happened once and hasn't come back."**

Bladder cancer bleeds intermittently. In the DETECT study the most common reason for referral was that the patient no longer had visible blood at the time of the appointment. A symptom that stopped is not a symptom that resolved.

**"I took antibiotics and it cleared up."**

The American College of Physicians notes that two months of symptoms despite appropriate antibiotics effectively rules out a simple UTI as the cause. They also note that repeated antibiotic courses delay the cancer diagnosis in men whose haematuria turned out to be malignant.

## The treatments

Treatment here is not one thing. It depends entirely on which cause is behind the blood, and for the two presentations — visible and microscopic — the pathways are entirely different.

### If it is an infection

Antibiotics, and it usually resolves. This is the one case where a straightforward treatment fixes the blood.

If the infection is prostatitis, the antibiotic choice also targets the prostate, because ordinary antibiotics penetrate it poorly. Some men get a longer course. Some men with recurrent bacterial prostatitis are kept on low-dose antibiotics to prevent relapse.

### If it is a stone

Most stones pass on their own. The treatment is about pain relief and, when it is needed, removing the stone.

For a stone that is small and in the upper ureter, watchful waiting with pain control is often the plan. For larger stones, or ones that have not moved after a period of observation, the options are **shock wave lithotripsy** (breaking it up from outside the body), **ureteroscopy** (a scope passed up from the bladder), or **percutaneous nephrolithotomy** (access through the back). All three are routine, all are done as day cases or overnight stays.

The blood usually stops once the stone has passed or been removed.

### If it is an enlarged prostate

The blood is a symptom of the obstruction, so treating the obstruction treats the blood.

**Alpha-blockers** relax the smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck, which improves flow quickly. **5-alpha reductase inhibitors** — finasteride and dutasteride — shrink the gland over months rather than hours, and are the drugs with the strongest data on preventing acute urinary retention. They also take roughly six months to work, which surprises people.

**Urology procedures** — including the HoLEP laser enucleation and TURP — remove or reshape the obstructing tissue. These are appropriate when medication is not enough, or when the gland is very large.

The important caveat, again: **treatment for BPH is not a substitute for working up the blood.** If the blood is visible, the cause still has to be proven rather than assumed.

### If it is prostatitis or CPPS

Here the treatment is quite different, because the blood is usually not the main problem.

**Antibiotics** for confirmed bacterial prostatitis, and the same point as above — for CPPS without bacterial infection, antibiotics do not outperform placebo.

**Anti-inflammatory medication** helps some men, though the AUA's position is that the evidence is *insufficient* to support treating CPPS as primarily an inflammatory condition.

**The best-supported treatment for CPPS is physical.** Pelvic floor physical therapy — assessment by palpation, manual release of trigger points, and retraining the muscles to stop guarding — has stronger evidence behind it than any drug. Trigger points are confirmed in roughly half to 57% of men with CPPS, and when they are released the referring pain usually eases with them.

**Bladder training and lifestyle measures** — reducing caffeine, alcohol and spicy food, managing fluid intake, and avoiding prolonged sitting — help many men.

### If it might be cancer

The treatment depends on the stage, and that is a conversation with a urologist rather than something to outline here.

The important part is the pathway: **cystoscopy to look directly at the bladder**, **upper tract imaging** with CT urography to examine the kidneys and ureters, and then biopsy during the cystoscopy if something is found. Blood tests like PSA may be part of the picture but they do not diagnose bladder cancer.

### If it is kidney disease

That is a nephrologist's territory, and treatment depends on which disease it is — blood pressure control, diabetes management, and in some cases specific immune or genetic treatments. The point for this article is that tea-coloured urine with protein and swelling needs that workup, not reassurance.

### If you found microscopic blood on a routine form

Much calmer, and the pathway is different.

- **A dipstick on its own is not enough.** At least **20% of dipstick-positive samples have no red blood cells on microscopy**. The proper test is a count, and the AUA defines microscopic haematuria as **3 or more red blood cells per high-power field** in a properly collected sample.
- **Your risk group depends on age, history and risk factors** — smoking, occupational chemical exposure, previous urological cancer, cyclophosphamide, family history. The AUA splits men into three: **low risk (0–0.4% malignancy)**, **intermediate (0.2–3.1%)**, **high (1.3–6.3%)**.
- **Any history of visible blood puts you in the high-risk group**, whatever the microscope shows now.
- **If the cause was an infection or a stone, the repeat test is what settles it.** After the infection clears, the urine gets retested. Red cells gone, you can be discharged. Red cells still there, you go on to full evaluation.

### If you have prostatitis or CPPS and visible blood

This is worth setting out properly, because it is where a plausible explanation meets a hard rule.

Inflammation in the prostate *can* cause blood in urine. If you have prostatitis and your urine is pink, that is a legitimate explanation. It is not a sufficient one.

The guidance for infection-related haematuria is that after the infection resolves, the urine is retested. If the red cells have cleared, the cause is established. If they persist, you continue to full evaluation for malignancy.

The reason to hold that line: men with lower urinary tract symptoms and prostate trouble are precisely the group whose haematuria gets attributed to the prostatitis and never properly worked up. **Prostatitis and BPH do not exclude cancer. They compete with it as explanations.**

### The practical mechanics

![Urine specimen cup and urinalysis dipstick on a laboratory bench](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794714303_d6db3822.png)

**Midstream clean-catch matters.** First urine of the day is concentrated and more likely to show things that are not there. It is the difference between a useful result and a false alarm.

**Dipstick positivity needs microscopy to back it up.** This is the 20% point above, and it is why "blood on dipstick" is not automatically haematuria.

**If you were exercising**, rest and repeat. Blood that persists at rest is not the exercise.

## Where this ends up

Visible blood in urine needs prompt urologic evaluation with cystoscopy, even when it stopped on its own. Thirty to forty per cent cancer risk, and a documented penalty for delay. A study of 4,000 men found a **34% increase in cancer-specific mortality** when diagnosis was delayed beyond nine months. That is not alarmism, it is just the arithmetic every guideline arrives at.

Microscopic blood on a routine form is a different and much calmer problem. Often under 1% risk in a young man with no risk factors, frequently needing nothing more than a repeat test.

And if you have prostatitis or CPPS, it may well be the explanation for your blood. But it has to be proven rather than assumed, because "I have prostatitis" is not an answer to "why is there blood in my urine". It is a hypothesis that needs testing, like any other.
