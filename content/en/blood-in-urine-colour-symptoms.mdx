## The symptom: you saw red, pink, or brown in the toilet

It catches you by accident. You glance down and the water is pink. Or it's the colour of weak tea. Or it's faintly rust-coloured, and you convince yourself you ate beetroot.

Blood in urine is called **haematuria**. If you can see it with the naked eye, it is **gross haematuria**. If it can only be found under a microscope, it is **microscopic haematuria**.

Here is the single most important thing to know about it, and it is the thing most health writing gets wrong:

> **These are two completely different problems. Visible blood in urine carries a 30–40% chance of a urinary tract cancer. Microscopic blood found incidentally carries about 3%.** The colour of what you see, and whether you saw it at all, changes what should happen next more than any other detail in this article.

If you can see blood, this article is for you. If you found it on a routine test form, skip to the section further down — because your situation is much less alarming and much less likely to be cancer.

## The cause: the colour tells you where it came from

### First: what the colour means

![Male urinary system cross-section showing kidneys, ureters, bladder and prostate, with two highlighted bleeding paths](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794706881_2a7101ef.png)

Blood in urine is not one thing. It is blood from somewhere in the urinary tract, and the colour narrows down where:

| What you see | Likely source |
|---|---|
| **Bright red / pink** | Lower tract — bladder, prostate, urethra |
| **Tea / cola / brown** | Upper tract — kidneys, ureters |
| **Rust or smoky** | Either, or old blood; also certain foods and drugs |
| **Clear, then pink on the paper** | Bleeding at the very end of urination — usually prostate or urethra |

Two important caveats. **Food and medication can change urine colour** without any blood at all — beetroot, blackberries, rhubarb, and some vitamin supplements do it reliably. And **no colour is a guarantee**. A bladder cancer can bleed bright red, and a kidney stone can produce surprisingly light-coloured urine.

So colour is a useful clue, not a diagnosis. Which is exactly why the next section matters more than this one.

### The causes, in order of how likely they are to explain your case

**1. Urinary tract infection.** The most common cause by a wide margin, and the most treatable. It typically comes with **burning on urination, frequency, urgency, and pain in the lower abdomen**. Bacteria, blood, and pus in the urine.

**2. Kidney stones.** Stones cause blood in around **20–25% of cases**, and in the emergency department, roughly **one in five urology referrals for blood in urine is a stone**. The blood is usually **visible**, and classically comes with **severe colicky pain in the loin or groin that comes in waves**. A stone that has moved into the bladder can cause visible blood with little or no pain at all.

**3. An enlarged prostate (BPH).** Common in men over 50. The enlarged gland can stretch the veins in the bladder neck, producing visible blood. This is why **BPH is on the AUA's list of causes of microscopic haematuria**.

**4. Prostatitis and chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS).** Your area of interest, and relevant here. Inflammation in and around the prostate can cause blood in urine, and it frequently does — particularly in acute bacterial prostatitis. It typically comes with **urination pain, frequency, painful ejaculation, and pelvic or perineal discomfort**.

**5. Bladder or kidney cancer.** Uncommon overall, but it is the reason visible blood matters so much. **Roughly 30–40% of visible haematuria turns out to be a urinary tract cancer** — this figure is consistent across the American College of Physicians, the American College of Radiology, and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. The DETECT study, which the AUA guideline cites, found that **13.2% of patients referred with visible haematuria had a urinary tract cancer, against 3.1% of those referred with microscopic haematuria**.

**6. Kidney disease.** Glomerular problems — the kidney's filtering units — produce **tea or cola-coloured urine**, often with **protein in the urine, swelling in the legs, or high blood pressure**. The AUA lists this as a cause of microscopic haematuria.

**7. A blood thinner.** More on this below, because it is the most commonly misread.

**8. Exercise.** Strenuous exercise, particularly running, can cause temporary blood in urine. It typically **resolves within 48 hours**. But — and this matters — it must be *confirmed* by a clean repeat test, not assumed. Bladder cancer also causes intermittent bleeding, and "I was running last week" is exactly the story that delays a diagnosis.

### The traps that delay diagnoses

**"I'm on warfarin, so it must be the blood thinner."**

This is the most damaging misreading, and the evidence is direct. A systematic review found that among patients on anticoagulants who presented with haematuria, **24% had a urinary tract malignancy**. The anticoagulant does not create the bleeding source — it prevents the clot that would otherwise stop it, and makes an existing source visible. The evaluation proceeds exactly the same way.

**"My prostate is enlarged, that's obviously the cause."**

BPH is a plausible cause, and it's on the guideline list. But it cannot be *assumed*. The AUA is explicit that gross haematuria in the setting of BPH must be **proven prostatic in origin** by appropriate evaluation, because the alternative explanation is the one that matters.

**"It happened once and hasn't come back."**

Bladder cancer bleeds intermittently. The DETECT study found that in patients referred with visible haematuria, the **most common reason for referral was no longer having visible blood at the time of the appointment**. A symptom that stopped is not a symptom that resolved.

**"I took antibiotics and it cleared up."**

The American College of Physicians notes that **two months of symptoms despite appropriate antibiotic treatment effectively rules out a simple urinary tract infection** as the cause — and that repeated antibiotic courses delay the cancer diagnosis in men whose haematuria turned out to be malignant.

## The result: what actually happens next

This is the part that varies most, and it's where the two presentations genuinely diverge.

### If you can see blood in your urine

**This needs prompt urologic evaluation.** Not "eventually." Promptly.

The pathway for visible haematuria is **cystoscopy and upper tract imaging** — a camera test of the bladder and an imaging test of the kidneys and ureters. The AUA guideline is clear on this:

- **Cystoscopy is mandatory.** Imaging alone cannot exclude bladder cancer, because bladder cancer is a surface lesion that imaging can miss. CT urography is highly sensitive for upper tract cancer, but it does not replace the camera.
- **The evaluation proceeds even if the episode was one-off and self-limited.** "I haven't seen it since" is not a reason to skip the cystoscopy.
- **Risk stratification is not optional** in the older or higher-risk reader, because the AUA risk tables assign very different workups to different risk groups.

A study of 4,000 men found a **34% increase in cancer-specific mortality when diagnosis was delayed beyond nine months**. That number is the reason this article exists.

### If you found microscopic blood on a test form

Much less alarming, and the pathway is different.

- **A dipstick alone is not enough.** At least **20% of dipstick-positive samples have no red blood cells on microscopy**. The proper test is a **microscopy count**, and the AUA definition of microscopic haematuria is **3 or more red blood cells per high-power field** in a properly collected specimen.
- **Your risk group is determined by your age, your history, and your risk factors** — smoking, occupational chemical exposure, prior urological cancer, cyclophosphamide, and family history. The AUA's risk tables produce three groups: **low risk (0–0.4% malignancy risk)**, **intermediate (0.2–3.1%)**, and **high (1.3–6.3%)**.
- **Any history of visible blood puts you in the high-risk group**, regardless of what the microscope shows at the time.
- **If the cause was an infection or a stone, the repeat test after it resolves matters.** The AUA requires a repeat urinalysis to confirm the blood has cleared. If it has, you can be discharged. If it hasn't, you proceed to full evaluation.

### The specific situation of a man with prostatitis or CPPS

This is worth stating plainly, because it is where a reasonable-looking explanation meets a hard rule.

Inflammation in the prostate **can** cause blood in urine. If you have prostatitis or CPPS and your urine is pink, that is a genuinely plausible explanation — but it is **not a sufficient one**.

The guidance for infection-related haematuria is that **after the infection resolves, the urine is retested**. If the red cells have cleared, the cause is established. If they persist, the patient continues to full evaluation for malignancy.

The reason to be strict here is that men with lower urinary tract symptoms and a background of prostate trouble are precisely the group most likely to have their haematuria attributed to the prostatitis and never properly worked up. **Prostatitis and BPH do not exclude cancer. They compete with it as explanations.**

### What to do about the things you can control

**A urine sample cup, a microscope slide, and a dipstick strip](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794706950_fa101aa0.png)

The practical mechanics of this, briefly:

- **A midstream clean-catch sample** is what makes the difference between a useful result and a false alarm. The first urine of the day is concentrated and more likely to show things that aren't there.
- **Dipstick positivity needs microscopy confirmation.** This is the 20% point above, and it's why a "blood on dipstick" result isn't automatically haematuria.
- **If it's a stone**, you'll usually know — the pain is hard to mistake. But a stone that has passed into the bladder can bleed without much pain.
- **If you're exercising**, rest and repeat. If the blood persists at rest, it's not the exercise.

### The bottom line

**Visible blood in your urine needs prompt urologic evaluation, with cystoscopy, even if it stopped on its own.** The reason is a 30–40% cancer risk and a documented mortality penalty for delay. That is not alarmism — it is arithmetic, and it is the same arithmetic every guideline agrees on.

**Microscopic blood found on a routine form is a different and much calmer problem**, usually under 1% risk in a low-risk younger man, and often requiring nothing more than a repeat test.

And if you have prostatitis or CPPS: it may well be the explanation. But the blood still has to be *proven* to come from the inflammation, because "I have prostatitis" is not an answer to "why is there blood in my urine" — it is a hypothesis that needs testing, like any other.
