## The symptoms: what you're actually seeing

You glance down and the water is pink. Or brown, like weak tea. Or faintly rust-coloured, and you spend a while wondering whether you ate beetroot yesterday.

The name for this is **haematuria**. Visible blood is **gross haematuria**; blood that takes a microscope to find is **microscopic haematuria**.

Those two get treated as one topic almost everywhere, and that is a mistake. The numbers are not close. **Visible blood carries a 30–40% chance of a urinary tract cancer.** Blood found incidentally on a routine urinalysis carries about 3% — under 1% in a young man with no risk factors.

So work out which one you have before reading further.

### The colour points to the source

![The colour range of urine, from light yellow through amber and brown to red](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794730609_ed20a891.jpg)

| What you see | Where it usually came from |
|---|---|
| Bright red, pink | Bladder, prostate, urethra |
| Tea, cola, brown | Kidney, ureter |
| Rust or smoky | Either, or old blood. Also food and some drugs |
| Pink only on the last few drops | Prostate or urethra |

Beetroot, blackberries, rhubarb and some vitamin supplements change urine colour with no blood involved at all. And colour is not a guarantee in the other direction either — bladder cancer can bleed bright red, and a stone can produce surprisingly pale urine.

Useful clue, not a diagnosis. Which is why what happens next matters more than what the colour suggests.

### The symptoms that come with it

Usually more informative than the colour.

| Alongside the blood | Points to |
|---|---|
| Burning, frequency, urgency, lower abdominal pain | Infection |
| Severe colicky pain in side or groin, in waves | Stone |
| Poor flow, weak stream, difficulty emptying | Prostate — BPH or prostatitis |
| Painful ejaculation, perineal or pelvic discomfort, sitting pain | Prostatitis or CPPS |
| Swollen ankles, protein in urine, high blood pressure | Kidney disease |
| Nothing at all | The one to take most seriously |

## The causes

**Urinary tract infection.** By a wide margin the most common cause, and the easiest to treat. Comes with burning, frequency, urgency, and usually lower abdominal pain.

**Kidney stones.** Stones cause bleeding in about 20–25% of cases, and roughly one in five urology referrals for blood in urine turns out to be a stone.

![Kidney cross-section showing stones in the calyces, renal pelvis and upper ureter](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794730619_9d2effd7.jpg)

**Enlarged prostate.** Common over 50. The stretched veins at the bladder neck bleed. This is why BPH is on the AUA's list of causes of microscopic haematuria.

**Prostatitis and CPPS.** Inflammation in and around the prostate causes blood in urine, and in acute bacterial prostatitis it frequently does. Comes with urination pain, frequency, painful ejaculation, and pelvic or perineal discomfort.

**Bladder or kidney cancer.** Uncommon overall, and the reason visible blood matters. Roughly 30–40% of visible haematuria is a urinary tract cancer — a figure that holds across the ACP, ACR and NCCN. The DETECT study, which the AUA guideline leans on, found **13.2%** malignancy in patients referred with visible blood against **3.1%** in those referred with microscopic blood.

![Three-dimensional cross-section of the male bladder and prostate gland](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794880362_cb407419.jpg)

**Kidney disease.** Problems with the glomeruli produce tea or cola-coloured urine, often with protein in the urine, swollen legs, or high blood pressure.

**A blood thinner.** See below — this one gets badly misread.

**Exercise.** Strenuous running can cause temporary blood that clears within about 48 hours. It needs confirming with a clean repeat test, not assuming. Bladder cancer also bleeds intermittently.

### Four mistakes that delay diagnoses

**"I'm on warfarin, so it's the blood thinner."** This one worries me most. A systematic review found that among patients on anticoagulants who presented with haematuria, **24% had a urinary tract malignancy**. A blood thinner does not create the bleeding — it stops the clot that would otherwise seal it, so an existing source becomes visible. The workup is the same either way.

**"My prostate is enlarged, that's obviously it."** BPH is a reasonable explanation and it's on the guideline list. It still cannot be assumed. The AUA is explicit that gross haematuria in a man with BPH has to be *proven* prostatic in origin, because the alternative is the one that matters.

**"It happened once and hasn't come back."** Bladder cancer bleeds intermittently. In DETECT, the most common reason for referral was that the patient no longer had visible blood at the time of the appointment. A symptom that stopped is not a symptom that resolved.

**"I took antibiotics and it cleared up."** The ACP notes that two months of symptoms despite appropriate antibiotics effectively rules out a simple UTI as the cause, and that repeated antibiotic courses delay the cancer diagnosis in men whose haematuria turned out to be malignant.

## The treatments

Treatment depends on the cause, and the two presentations follow entirely different pathways.

### Infection

Antibiotics, and it usually resolves. The one case where a straightforward treatment fixes the blood.

If the infection is prostatitis, the antibiotic also has to reach the prostate, because ordinary antibiotics penetrate it poorly. Some men need a longer course, and some with recurrent bacterial prostatitis are kept on low-dose antibiotics to prevent relapse.

### Stone

Most stones pass on their own. The treatment is pain relief and, when needed, removing it.

Small stones in the upper ureter are usually watched with pain control. Larger ones, or ones that have not moved, are dealt with by **shock wave lithotripsy** (breaking it up from outside), **ureteroscopy** (a scope passed up from the bladder), or **percutaneous nephrolithotomy** (access through the back). All routine, all day cases or overnight. The blood usually stops once the stone has passed or been removed.

### Enlarged prostate

Treat the obstruction, and the blood improves with it.

**Alpha-blockers** relax the smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck, improving flow quickly. **5-alpha reductase inhibitors** — finasteride and dutasteride — shrink the gland over months, and have the strongest data on preventing acute urinary retention. They take about six months to work, which surprises people.

**Urology procedures** — HoLEP and TURP — remove or reshape the obstructing tissue when medication isn't enough or the gland is very large.

The caveat again: **treatment for BPH is not a substitute for working up the blood.** If the blood is visible, the cause still has to be proven.

### Prostatitis or CPPS

Here the blood is usually not the main problem, and the treatment looks quite different.

**Antibiotics** for confirmed bacterial prostatitis. For CPPS without bacterial infection, antibiotics do not outperform placebo.

**Anti-inflammatory medication** helps some men, though the AUA's position is that the evidence is *insufficient* to support treating CPPS as primarily an inflammatory condition.

**Pelvic floor physical therapy** has the strongest evidence of any CPPS treatment — assessment by palpation, manual release of trigger points, and retraining the muscles to stop guarding. Trigger points are confirmed in roughly half to 57% of men with CPPS, and when released the referring pain usually eases with them.

**Bladder training and lifestyle measures** — less caffeine, alcohol and spicy food, managed fluid intake, avoiding prolonged sitting — help many men.

### If it might be cancer

Treatment depends on stage, and that is a conversation with a urologist rather than something to outline here. The pathway matters more: **cystoscopy** to look directly at the bladder, **CT urography** for the kidneys and ureters, and biopsy during the cystoscopy if something is found. PSA may be part of the picture but does not diagnose bladder cancer.

### Kidney disease

A nephrologist's territory, and treatment depends on which disease it is — blood pressure control, diabetes management, and in some cases specific immune or genetic treatments. Tea-coloured urine with protein and swelling needs that workup, not reassurance.

### If you found microscopic blood on a routine form

Much calmer, different pathway.

- **A dipstick alone is not enough.** At least **20% of dipstick-positive samples have no red blood cells on microscopy**. The proper test is a count, and the AUA defines microscopic haematuria as **3 or more red blood cells per high-power field** in a properly collected sample.
- **Your risk group depends on age, history and risk factors** — smoking, occupational chemical exposure, previous urological cancer, cyclophosphamide, family history. The AUA splits men into three: **low risk (0–0.4%)**, **intermediate (0.2–3.1%)**, **high (1.3–6.3%)**.
- **Any history of visible blood puts you in the high-risk group**, whatever the microscope shows now.
- **If the cause was an infection or a stone, the repeat test settles it.** After the infection clears, the urine is retested. Red cells gone, discharged. Red cells still there, full evaluation.

### If you have prostatitis or CPPS and visible blood

Worth setting out properly, because it is where a plausible explanation meets a hard rule.

Inflammation in the prostate *can* cause blood in urine. If you have prostatitis and your urine is pink, that is a legitimate explanation. It is not a sufficient one.

The guidance for infection-related haematuria is that after the infection resolves, the urine is retested. Red cells cleared, the cause is established. Red cells persisting, you continue to full evaluation for malignancy.

The reason to hold that line: men with lower urinary tract symptoms and prostate trouble are precisely the group whose haematuria gets attributed to the prostatitis and never properly worked up. **Prostatitis and BPH do not exclude cancer. They compete with it as explanations.**

### Practicalities

![Urine specimen cup and urinalysis dipstick on a laboratory bench](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794880347_b87ae689.jpg)

**Midstream clean-catch matters.** First urine of the day is concentrated and more likely to show things that aren't there.

**Dipstick positivity needs microscopy.** That's the 20% point above, and why "blood on dipstick" is not automatically haematuria.

**If you were exercising**, rest and repeat. Blood that persists at rest is not the exercise.

## Where this ends up

Visible blood needs prompt urologic evaluation with cystoscopy, even when it stopped on its own. A study of 4,000 men found a **34% increase in cancer-specific mortality** when diagnosis was delayed beyond nine months. That isn't alarmism — it's the arithmetic every guideline arrives at.

Microscopic blood on a routine form is a different and much calmer problem. Often under 1% risk in a young man with no risk factors, frequently needing nothing more than a repeat test.

And if you have prostatitis or CPPS, it may well be the explanation for your blood. But it has to be proven rather than assumed, because "I have prostatitis" is not an answer to "why is there blood in my urine". It is a hypothesis that needs testing, like any other.
