You glance down and the water is pink. Or brown, like weak tea. Or faintly rust-coloured, and you spend a while wondering whether you ate beetroot yesterday.

The name for this is **haematuria**. If you can see it with the naked eye it is **gross haematuria**. If it takes a microscope to find it, it is **microscopic haematuria**.

Those two are treated as one topic almost everywhere, and that is a mistake. The numbers are not close.

**Visible blood in urine carries a 30–40% chance of a urinary tract cancer.** Blood found incidentally on a routine urinalysis carries about 3%. In a young man with no risk factors, well under 1%.

So before anything else, work out which of those two you are dealing with. If you can see it, keep reading. If a form told you about it, the second half of this article is the one for you.

## What the colour is telling you

![Male urinary system showing the kidneys, ureters, bladder and prostate](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/cdn_upload/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794714698_c218292a.jpg)

Blood in urine is not a diagnosis. It is a symptom, and it came from somewhere in the urinary tract. The colour narrows down where.

| What you see | Where it usually came from |
|---|---|
| Bright red, pink | Bladder, prostate, urethra |
| Tea, cola, brown | Kidney, ureter |
| Rust or smoky | Either, or old blood. Also food and some drugs |
| Pink only on the last few drops | Prostate or urethra |

Two caveats, because people get caught out by both.

Food and medication change urine colour with no blood involved at all. Beetroot, blackberries and rhubarb do it reliably, as do some vitamin supplements. And colour is not a guarantee in the other direction either. Bladder cancer can bleed bright red, and a kidney stone can produce surprisingly pale urine.

Useful clue, not a diagnosis. Which is exactly why what happens next matters more than what the colour suggests.

## The causes, roughly in order of how often they turn out to be right

**Urinary tract infection.** By a wide margin the most common cause, and the easiest to deal with. It comes with burning when you pass urine, going more often, urgency, and usually pain low in the abdomen.

**Kidney stones.** Stones cause bleeding in about 20–25% of cases. Roughly one in five urology referrals for blood in urine turns out to be a stone. Expect severe colicky pain in the side or groin, coming in waves. One caveat: a stone that has worked its way into the bladder can bleed noticeably with little or no pain.

**Enlarged prostate.** Common over 50. The stretched veins at the bladder neck bleed, and it is on the AUA's list of causes of microscopic haematuria.

**Prostatitis and CPPS.** Inflammation in and around the prostate causes blood in urine, and in acute bacterial prostatitis it is quite common. It comes with painful urination, frequency, painful ejaculation, and perineal or pelvic discomfort.

**Cancer.** Uncommon overall, and the reason visible blood gets taken seriously. Somewhere between 30 and 40% of visible haematuria turns out to be a urinary tract cancer — a figure that holds across the American College of Physicians, the American College of Radiology and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network. The DETECT study, which the AUA guideline leans on, found 13.2% malignancy in patients referred with visible blood against 3.1% in those referred with microscopic blood.

**Kidney disease.** Problems with the glomeruli, the filtering units of the kidney, produce tea-coloured urine, often alongside protein in the urine, swollen ankles or high blood pressure.

**Blood thinners.** See below, because this one gets badly misread.

**Exercise.** Strenuous running can cause temporary blood in urine that clears within about 48 hours. It needs confirming with a clean repeat test rather than assuming, because bladder cancer also bleeds intermittently.

## Four mistakes that delay diagnoses

**"I'm on warfarin, so it's the blood thinner."**

This is the one that worries me most. A systematic review found that among patients on anticoagulants who presented with haematuria, 24% had a urinary tract malignancy. A blood thinner does not create the bleeding. It stops the clot that would otherwise seal it, so an existing source becomes visible. The workup is the same either way.

**"My prostate is enlarged, that's obviously it."**

BPH is a reasonable explanation and it is on the guideline list. It still cannot be assumed. The AUA is explicit that gross haematuria in a man with BPH has to be *proven* prostatic in origin, because the alternative is the one that matters.

**"It happened once and hasn't come back."**

Bladder cancer bleeds intermittently. In the DETECT study the most common reason for referral was that the patient no longer had visible blood at the time of the appointment. A symptom that stopped is not a symptom that resolved.

**"I took antibiotics and it cleared up."**

The American College of Physicians notes that two months of symptoms despite appropriate antibiotics effectively rules out a simple UTI as the cause. They also note that repeated antibiotic courses delay the cancer diagnosis in men whose haematuria turned out to be malignant.

## If you can see blood in your urine

This needs prompt urologic evaluation. Not "when I get round to it".

The pathway is **cystoscopy and upper tract imaging** — a camera test of the bladder and an imaging test of the kidneys and ureters. The AUA guideline is blunt about why:

- **Cystoscopy is not optional.** Imaging alone cannot exclude bladder cancer, because bladder cancer is a surface lesion and imaging can miss it. CT urography is very good at picking up upper tract cancer, but it does not replace the camera.
- **The workup goes ahead even if the episode was one-off.** "It hasn't happened since" is not a reason to skip the cystoscopy.
- **Risk stratification matters** in older or higher-risk men, because the AUA risk tables send very different people down very different paths.

A study of 4,000 men found a 34% increase in cancer-specific mortality when diagnosis was delayed beyond nine months. That is the reason to take visible blood seriously even when it feels like nothing.

## If you found it on a test form

Much calmer, and a different pathway.

- **A dipstick on its own is not enough.** At least 20% of dipstick-positive samples have no red blood cells on microscopy. The proper test is a count, and the AUA defines microscopic haematuria as **3 or more red blood cells per high-power field** in a properly collected sample.
- **Your risk group depends on age, history and risk factors** — smoking, occupational chemical exposure, previous urological cancer, cyclophosphamide, family history. The AUA splits men into three: low risk (0–0.4% malignancy), intermediate (0.2–3.1%), high (1.3–6.3%).
- **Any history of visible blood puts you in the high-risk group**, whatever the microscope shows now.
- **If the cause was an infection or a stone, the repeat test is what settles it.** After the infection clears, the urine gets retested. Red cells gone, you can be discharged. Red cells still there, you go on to full evaluation.

## If you have prostatitis or CPPS

This is worth setting out properly, because it is where a plausible explanation meets a hard rule.

Inflammation in the prostate can cause blood in urine. If you have prostatitis and your urine is pink, that is a legitimate explanation. It is not a sufficient one.

The guidance for infection-related haematuria is that after the infection resolves, the urine is retested. If the red cells have cleared, the cause is established. If they persist, you continue to full evaluation for malignancy.

The reason to hold that line: men with lower urinary tract symptoms and prostate trouble are precisely the group whose haematuria gets attributed to the prostatitis and never properly worked up. Prostatitis and BPH do not exclude cancer. They compete with it as explanations.

## The mechanics

![Urine specimen cup and urinalysis dipstick on a laboratory bench](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1794714303_d6db3822.png)

A few practical points.

**Midstream clean-catch matters.** First urine of the day is concentrated and more likely to show things that are not there. It is the difference between a useful result and a false alarm.

**Dipstick positivity needs microscopy to back it up.** This is the 20% point above, and it is why "blood on dipstick" is not automatically haematuria.

**If it is a stone**, you will usually know. The pain is hard to mistake. A stone that has passed into the bladder can bleed without much pain.

**If you were exercising**, rest and repeat. Blood that persists at rest is not the exercise.

## Where this ends up

Visible blood in urine needs prompt urologic evaluation with cystoscopy, even when it stopped on its own. Thirty to forty per cent cancer risk, and a documented penalty for delay. That is not alarmism, it is just the arithmetic every guideline arrives at.

Microscopic blood on a routine form is a different and much calmer problem. Often under 1% risk in a young man with no risk factors, frequently needing nothing more than a repeat test.

And if you have prostatitis or CPPS, it may well be the explanation for your blood. But it has to be proven rather than assumed, because "I have prostatitis" is not an answer to "why is there blood in my urine". It is a hypothesis that needs testing, like any other.
