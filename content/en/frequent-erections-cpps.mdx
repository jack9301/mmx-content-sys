## The question that brings you here

Look, this is one of those questions that comes up almost every week in my clinic, and I want to give you a real answer — not the textbook answer.

A guy sits across from me, sometimes a little embarrassed, and asks something like: *"Doc, I think I've been having erections too often. Could that be making this worse?"*

Or: *"I read online that ejaculation can help. But when I do it, I feel worse the next day. What's going on?"*

Or the one that worries me most: *"I've been avoiding sex because I'm scared it's making my prostatitis worse."*

All three of these are the same question, underneath. And all three deserve a serious answer.

So let me give you one. Not a research-paper answer. Not a "well, it depends" answer. A real answer, the kind I'd give you if you were sitting across from me right now.

## Let me give you the honest answer first

**Here's the honest truth:** Erections themselves do not cause chronic pelvic pain syndrome. They never have. No urologist who has spent any time with this condition believes that.

But — and this is the part that matters — the *way* you have erections, how often, how long, and what's happening in your pelvic floor during them, can absolutely aggravate an already-irritated prostate and pelvic floor. Especially if you're someone whose CPPS is driven by muscle tension, which most cases are.

So the answer isn't "stop." The answer is "pay attention."

## Here's what's actually happening inside your body

When you get an erection, three things happen at the same time:

1. **Your pelvic floor muscles contract.** The bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles wrap around the base of the penis and squeeze. This is normal — it's part of how an erection stays rigid.

2. **Your prostate swells with fluid.** The prostate, the seminal vesicles, and the Cowper's glands all ramp up production. There's increased blood flow and glandular activity in the area.

3. **Your nerve sensitivity goes up.** The pudendal nerve and the pelvic splanchnic nerves light up. The whole region becomes more sensitive — touch, pressure, temperature, all of it.

None of that is a problem in isolation. In a healthy pelvic floor, all three of those processes happen smoothly, you do your thing, everything relaxes again, and you move on.

But if your pelvic floor is already tight — which, if you have CPPS, it almost certainly is — those three processes are happening on top of an already-irritated system. And that's where the trouble starts.

## The connection between erections and CPPS

Here's the part most articles won't tell you: **it's not really about how often. It's about what happens to your pelvic floor during and after.**

In CPPS, the pelvic floor muscles — the levator ani, the obturator internus, the pubococcygeus, the others — are already in a heightened state. They're guarded. They're tender. Some of them may be in actual trigger-point spasm. When I examine patients in clinic, I can often feel these muscles as tight bands, sometimes as tender knots.

Now imagine those muscles being asked to do their erection-contraction work. They contract hard, repeatedly, for however long the erection lasts. And then, in many CPPS patients, instead of fully relaxing back down afterward, they stay partly contracted. They hold tension. The next erection comes, they contract again, and they hold tension again. Layer by layer, the resting tone climbs.

Add to that the prostate swelling with fluid, the nerves staying sensitized, and the cumulative fatigue in muscles that don't fully recover between sessions — and you have a setup for a flare-up.

The prostate itself isn't damaged by erection. It's not "overworked." But the tissues around it — the muscles, the fascia, the nerves — can absolutely get pushed past their recovery threshold. And when they do, you feel it. Perineum pain. Sitting pain. Urinary urgency. The whole constellation.

## What "too much" actually means

A "marathon session" is what I call any pattern of sexual activity — partnered or solo — that goes well past your normal duration or frequency in a way your body can't recover from. That might be:

- A solo session that lasts 1-2+ hours, especially with extended edging (delaying orgasm)
- Multiple ejaculations within a few hours
- Daily long sessions for several days in a row
- Hours of porn-driven stimulation without a real refractory break
- Frequent sessions combined with gripping/tightening the pelvic floor as part of arousal (very common and very hard on those muscles)

It's not a number. Some men can have sex twice a day and feel fine. Others can have one session a week and feel wiped out for two days after. The right question isn't "how many times is too many" — it's **"how do I feel the next morning, and the morning after that?"**

If you feel fine, your body is handling it. If you feel worse — more perineal pressure, more urinary frequency, more sitting pain, more of that low-grade pelvic ache — your body is telling you something. **Listen to it.**

I'm not here to lecture you about porn. That's not my job, and you didn't come to me for that. But I am here to tell you that the pattern matters, and if your symptoms are worse, the pattern is worth looking at honestly.

## What the research shows (and what it doesn't)

Let me be honest about what the literature actually says, because a lot of online articles oversell this.

The AUA 2025 CPPS guideline mentions sexual dysfunction as a domain of the UPOINT phenotype system and notes that ejaculatory pain is a common feature of CPPS. It also states clearly that sexual activity is not contraindicated in CPPS — in fact, regular ejaculation is often recommended as part of conservative management.

Anderson et al. 2006 looked at a cohort of men with chronic prostatitis symptoms and found that higher NIH-CPSI scores correlated with reports of more frequent ejaculation. That sounds alarming, but here's the catch: **it's correlation, not causation.** Men with worse CPPS may have more frequent ejaculation as a coping mechanism, or as an attempt to "clear out" the prostate, or because they're anxious and masturbating to manage anxiety. The study can't tell us which direction the arrow goes.

Clemens and colleagues have done good work showing that pelvic floor muscle dysfunction is a primary driver of CPPS symptoms in many men, and that anything that loads those muscles without recovery — including sexual activity — can be a contributing factor.

Nickel's 2011 UPOINT framework explicitly lists sexual dysfunction as one of the six phenotype domains, recognizing that for some men, sexual activity patterns are a meaningful part of the clinical picture.

So the honest reading of the evidence: **yes, the connection is real, and yes, it shows up in clinic. But we don't have a clean RCT showing "X erections per week cause Y% symptom worsening." Anyone who tells you they have that study is making it up.**

## What I tell my patients in clinic

Here are the practical things I actually tell men in my office. These aren't theory. They're what I say across the desk, in the room with the door closed.

1. **Stay sexually active. Do not avoid sex.** Avoidance makes CPPS worse. Muscles that are guarded and not used become more guarded. A regular, healthy sexual life is part of the recovery plan, not a barrier to it.

2. **Notice your patterns, not your count.** If you have a marathon session and feel worse the next day, that's information. The fix isn't to never do that again. The fix is to know that's your recovery threshold, and to give yourself a few days after before doing it again.

3. **Stop aggressive edging as a habit.** There's nothing wrong with edging occasionally. But if your default pattern is "stay on the edge for 45 minutes, then finish," you're asking your pelvic floor to hold a sustained contraction for a long time. Vary the rhythm. Let arousal build and release. Don't treat orgasm as the finish line of a long holding pattern.

4. **Breathe during sex.** Sounds silly, but I mean it. Many men with CPPS hold their breath or breath-hold during arousal and orgasm. The Valsalva maneuver spikes intra-abdominal pressure and tenses the pelvic floor. Consciously slow your breathing. Let the exhale happen. You'll be surprised how much this changes the post-orgasm pelvic floor state.

5. **Don't grip hard during masturbation.** If you're using a very tight grip, you're training the pelvic floor to contract at high intensity for prolonged periods. Loosen the grip. Use less force. The pelvic floor is doing the work either way; you're adding unnecessary load.

6. **After sex, do 60 seconds of pelvic floor drops.** A "drop" or "reverse Kegel" is the opposite of a Kegel — you gently bear down as if starting to urinate, letting the pelvic floor fully lengthen and release. Sixty seconds of this, with slow breathing, helps the muscles return to baseline. Do it lying down if you can.

7. **If a particular position makes symptoms worse, change it.** Some men find that certain positions put more perineal pressure on the prostate. Prone (lying face down) is often one. If a position makes you feel worse during or after, pick a different one. Your body is giving you feedback.

8. **Watch the alcohol and the porn combination.** Alcohol numbs the early-warning signals of pelvic floor fatigue. Porn extends the session. The combination reliably produces marathon sessions in men who wouldn't otherwise have them. Be honest with yourself about this.

## The other side: avoiding sex makes CPPS worse

I want to close with the most important thing, because I see this go wrong more often than the "too much" problem.

A lot of men read something like this article, and their takeaway is: "I should stop having sex to protect my prostate." That is the wrong takeaway. In fact, it's the opposite of what I want you to do.

When you avoid sexual activity:

- Your pelvic floor muscles don't get the periodic full contraction-and-release cycle they need
- The prostate fluid doesn't get regularly expressed, and congestion can build up
- The anxiety around your symptoms goes up, which makes the muscles more guarded
- You start to associate your body with fear, and that itself is a driver of chronic pain

The patients I see who recover fastest are the ones who **return to a normal, healthy sexual life as soon as they can** — not the ones who white-knuckle through abstinence thinking they're being good patients.

The answer isn't "stop." The answer isn't "push through pain." The answer is **"pay attention, modify the patterns that hurt you, and stay in the game."**

That's what I tell my patients. That's what I want to leave you with.
