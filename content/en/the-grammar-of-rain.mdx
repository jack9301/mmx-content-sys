English has one word for rain. Chinese has several. Japanese has even more.

In Mandarin, there is 雨 (yǔ) for the general idea, and 细雨 (xìyǔ) for the thin stuff, 暴雨 (bàoyǔ) for the violent stuff, 阵雨 (zhènyǔ) for the passing stuff, and梅雨 (méiyǔ) for the sticky stuff that arrives in June and stays for weeks. The language has decided these are different things worth distinguishing.

Japanese goes further. 霧雨 (kirisame) is rain so fine it is almost mist. 夕立 (yūdachi) is the sudden evening downpour of summer. 時雨 (shigure) is the cold, intermittent rain of late autumn. Each word is a small piece of weather knowledge, frozen in syllables.

## Why this matters

When a language gives you a word for something, you start noticing it. Before I knew 霧雨, I never distinguished fine rain from ordinary rain. After I knew it, I started seeing it everywhere — the particular silver of an autumn morning, the way the world looks slightly softer than it should.

A word is a kind of attention. Languages are attention-distribution machines. Some pay out to thick descriptions, others to thin ones. None are wrong. But they are not the same.

## A small experiment

For one week, try to notice the kinds of rain. Not just "it is raining" or "it is not." The weight of it. The temperature. The duration. The mood of the sky before it starts.

You will run out of English words. Borrow a few.
