Finasteride and dutasteride are the only medications proven to shrink an enlarged prostate. They are also the slowest-acting BPH treatments, and that combination causes most of the confusion around them. Men take them for three weeks, feel nothing, assume they don't work, and stop — then read online that they wrecked their sex life and never start again.

Both complaints are understandable and both are wrong.

Here's what actually happens when you put a 5-alpha-reductase inhibitor on board, based on the trial data rather than the marketing.

## What these drugs actually do

Your prostate grows under the influence of **dihydrotestosterone (DHT)** — a converted form of testosterone that binds the androgen receptor far more aggressively than testosterone itself. An enzyme called 5-alpha-reductase performs that conversion. There are two versions of the enzyme, and this is where the two drugs differ.

- **Finasteride blocks the type 2 isoenzyme.** It's the predominant form inside prostate tissue. Result: serum DHT falls about 70%.
- **Dutasteride blocks both type 1 and type 2.** Type 1 lives mostly in skin and liver. Result: serum DHT falls about 95%.

Blocking DHT removes the main growth signal driving benign prostatic hyperplasia. Over months, epithelial cells begin to undergo apoptosis and prostate volume contracts.

![Two abstract streams of shapes merging, one softly blocked by a pale barrier](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1792673055_9696c2fd.png)

**The distinction from alpha-blockers matters more than anything else on this page.** Tamsulosin and the other alpha-blockers occupy adrenergic receptors on the smooth muscle in your prostate and bladder neck, relaxing it within hours. The gland itself does not change size. Finasteride and dutasteride change the gland's size but take months to do it. They are not competing treatments. They address different parts of the same obstruction.

## What the long trials actually showed

The evidence here is unusually solid — these drugs have been studied for longer than most medical interventions.

**The PLESS trial** followed 3,040 men for four years and is the landmark finasteride study. Over that period:

- **Surgery risk fell 55%.** Ten percent of men on placebo needed an operation; 5% on finasteride did.
- **Acute urinary retention risk fell 57%.** Seven percent on placebo versus 3% on finasteride.
- **Prostate volume shrank about 18%,** while men on placebo saw theirs grow roughly 14%.
- **Maximum urinary flow improved by 1.9 mL/s** versus 0.2 mL/s on placebo.

Those are hard outcomes, not symptom scores. Acute urinary retention means being unable to urinate and needing a catheter — the specific event most feared by men with BPH.

**The MTOPS trial** randomized 3,047 men to placebo, doxazosin, finasteride, or both, and followed them for about 4.5 years. The result is the one that explains why combination therapy is standard of care:

| Treatment | Reduction in overall clinical progression |
|---|---|
| Doxazosin alone (alpha-blocker) | 39% |
| Finasteride alone (5-ARI) | 34% |
| **Both together** | **66%** |

But MTOPS also found something less publicized. When the researchers looked specifically at acute urinary retention and the need for invasive treatment, **finasteride reduced both. Doxazosin did not.** The alpha-blocker improved how men felt without protecting them from the events they most wanted to avoid.

That single finding is the whole argument for 5-ARIs in men with genuinely enlarged prostates.

## The 6-to-12-month problem

This is where most of the disappointment comes from, and it isn't a failure of the drug. It's a mismatch between how the drug works and how people assess medication.

Alpha-blockers work on hours. In trials, tamsulosin improved peak flow within 4 to 8 hours of the first dose. A man who wakes up able to urinate without straining concludes the medication is working. If that same man is also prescribed finasteride, he takes both, feels the benefit of the first one, and attributes it to both.

Finasteride works by triggering cellular apoptosis in prostate epithelium. That is a slow biological process. In the PLESS data, prostate volume changes became statistically clear around the 8-month mark and continued to hold. FDA labeling is explicit that most patients need at least 6 months of treatment before you can fairly judge symptomatic benefit, and the label notes that even men who improve early typically need that full window.

![Soft calendar page shapes drifting past one another, suggesting months passing](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1792673552_974d7c75.png)

**A useful reframe:** if you care about how you feel this week, judge alpha-blockers on weeks. If you care about whether you end up in an emergency room with a catheter at 2am five years from now, judge 5-ARIs on years. Both statements are true simultaneously, and the confusion comes from expecting one drug's timeline from the other.

## The sexual side effects, honestly

The data is clearer and less dramatic than the internet suggests.

From the PLESS trial's first year:

| Side effect | Finasteride | Placebo |
|---|---|---|
| Erectile dysfunction | 8.1% | 3.7% |
| Decreased libido | 6.4% | 3.4% |
| Ejaculation disorder | 0.8% | 0.1% |
| Reduced ejaculate volume | 3.7% | 0.8% |
| Breast enlargement | 0.5% | 0.1% |

That is the worst year. **In years two through four, the difference between the groups largely disappeared.** Cumulative rates for erectile dysfunction over the full four years were 5.1% in both arms. Most new-onset adverse events of any kind occur during year one, and after that the incidence tracks placebo.

In the one-year head-to-head EPICS trial comparing 1,630 men directly, the two drugs were statistically indistinguishable: impotence 7% versus 8%, decreased libido 5% versus 6%, gynecomastia 1% in both arms.

**On "post-finasteride syndrome":** this refers to reports of persistent sexual, cognitive, and mood symptoms after discontinuation. The term is not recognized as a distinct clinical diagnosis, and the AUA does not list it among established adverse effects. A small number of men report it, and dismissing those experiences is dismissive. But it has not been demonstrated at anything beyond anecdotal rates, and the trial populations above — thousands of men followed for years — did not show a syndrome emerging. If you stop the medication and your symptoms persist, that is worth raising with a clinician rather than attributing it silently to a drug you no longer take.

## The PSA problem, which is safety-critical

This is the part that genuinely matters, and the part most often handled badly.

Finasteride and dutasteride **reduce serum PSA by roughly 50%**. This is not a side effect. It's a direct consequence of less prostate epithelium producing PSA, and it's the mechanistic proof that the drug is working. Both drug labels state that for men treated six months or longer, **the measured PSA should be doubled** before comparison against normal reference ranges.

Consider a concrete scenario. A man takes finasteride for two years. His annual PSA comes back at 2.1 ng/mL. The lab's reference range is 0 to 4. It looks fine. By the labeling rule, his true level is closer to 4.2 — meaningfully different, and worth acting on.

The failures happen at handoffs. A new doctor, a different hospital, a different screening clinic. Nobody sees the medication list. The 2.1 is read as normal.

**What to do:** state that you're on a 5-ARI every single time you have PSA bloodwork, and make sure it's on your records. Know the doubling rule yourself rather than relying on the clinician to recall it.

One refinement from Marks and colleagues: doubling slightly overestimates actual PSA during years one to three and slightly underestimates it thereafter, which is why a rise of 0.3 ng/mL or more from your post-treatment nadir is treated as a reason to investigate further.

There is a related finding worth knowing. The Prostate Cancer Prevention Trial found finasteride reduced overall prostate cancer incidence by about 25% in healthy men, but increased the proportion of high-grade (Gleason 7-10) cancers — 1.8% versus 1.1%. Most experts attribute this to detection bias, since finasteride shrinks the gland and can mask or unmask lesions unpredictably. It's a reminder that these drugs were never designed as cancer prevention, and that anyone taking one should stay current with cancer screening rather than assume the drug has handled it.

## Finasteride versus dutasteride

| | Finasteride | Dutasteride |
|---|---|---|
| Enzymes blocked | Type 2 | Type 1 and 2 |
| Serum DHT reduction | ~70% | ~95% |
| Prostate volume reduction | ~18-20% | ~26-28% |
| Half-life | 6-8 hours | ~5 weeks |
| Generic available | Yes, widely | Limited in some markets |
| Sexual side effects | No significant difference from dutasteride | No significant difference from finasteride |
| Evidence base | Largest and longest | Large, fewer years |

The half-life difference is the practical one. Dutasteride stays in your system for roughly five weeks, which is why a missed day doesn't matter and why the effect takes longer to fully wash out after stopping. Finasteride clears in hours.

On efficacy, the differences are real but modest. Dutasteride suppresses DHT more completely and shrinks the gland slightly more, and in the CombAT trial its benefit was consistent across prostate volume strata where finasteride's benefit scaled with size. But the EPICS trial found the two clinically equivalent on symptom scores and flow rates. The choice usually comes down to availability, cost, and prescriber preference rather than a clear efficacy gap.

## What these drugs can't do

They work on benign tissue. That boundary matters.

- **They don't treat prostate cancer.** There's no established clinical benefit in cancer, and the high-grade detection finding above is a caution, not an indication.
- **They don't relieve prostatitis or CPPS symptoms.** These are different diseases with different mechanisms. If your symptoms are pelvic pain rather than urinary obstruction, these drugs won't help — and this is one of the most common diagnostic errors in BPH treatment.
- **They don't reverse established obstruction immediately.** If you're currently unable to urinate, that's an acute situation requiring catheterization, not a prescription to wait three months.
- **They don't eliminate the need for lifestyle work.** Fluid timing, caffeine, alcohol, and movement still affect symptoms on medication.

That third point about diagnosis deserves emphasis. BPH, prostatitis, and CPPS can present with overlapping urinary symptoms, and the treatments are not interchangeable. A man with pelvic pain and urinary frequency who gets prescribed finasteride and dutasteride, waits a year, and sees no improvement has not had a drug failure. He's had a misdiagnosis.

## Combination therapy and the two-drug timeline

![Two overlapping abstract treatment streams running toward a calm horizon](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1792672675_1b6b7b0b.png)

Guidelines recommend combining a 5-ARI with an alpha-blocker for men with demonstrably enlarged prostates. The rationale follows directly from the mechanism: the alpha-blocker relaxes the muscle quickly, the 5-ARI shrinks the gland slowly, and together they address both components of obstruction plus the long-term progression risk.

The CombAT trial enrolled 4,844 men with prostates over 30 mL and followed them four years. Combination therapy reduced overall clinical progression to 12.6%, versus 17.8% for dutasteride alone and 21.5% for tamsulosin alone.

There's a practical refinement that has emerged from the SMART trial. After six months of successful combination therapy, roughly three-quarters of men could discontinue the alpha-blocker without symptom deterioration. This makes pharmacological sense — once the gland has physically shrunk, there's less muscle left under tension. The exceptions are men who started with severe symptoms, who tend to need both drugs longer.

## How long to expect to be on it

This is a 5-to-10 year commitment, not a 6-month course. The AUA guideline frames 5-ARIs as long-term therapy for men with enlarged prostates, and stopping reverses the effect — the PLESS follow-up data show prostates regrow toward baseline after discontinuation, with urinary retention and surgery risk climbing back.

The people who do best on these drugs are the ones who understand that at the start. Set the expectation before the first pill: this is a years-long management decision, not a symptomatic relief course. The men who quit at week three and the men who stay for five years take the same drug — they just get different results.

If side effects become the reason you want to stop, that is a legitimate conversation to have with the prescriber. Options exist, including switching between the two drugs, dropping to a 5-ARI alone, or stopping entirely and accepting the progression risk. What matters is making that decision deliberately rather than by abandonment.

## Where lifestyle still fits

Medication doesn't make lifestyle irrelevant — it changes what lifestyle work is worth doing.

**Still worth doing:** fluid timing in the evening to reduce nocturia, moderating caffeine and alcohol which act as diuretics and bladder irritants, regular movement, and avoiding prolonged perineal pressure from sitting. These affect symptoms directly and complement any medication.

**Not worth doing:** chasing supplements marketed as prostate shrinkers. The evidence for saw palmetto and similar products is weak and inconsistent, and none of them shrink the gland the way these two drugs demonstrably do.

Once symptoms are controlled on medication, the highest-value lifestyle shift is often not a supplement or a diet change. It's consistency — the boring daily habits that keep urinary output predictable and pelvic floor relaxed.

## The bottom line

Finasteride and dutasteride do something no other BPH medication does: they change the physical size of the prostate and measurably reduce the chance of acute urinary retention and surgery. That benefit accrues over years and is invisible in the first weeks.

They cost you a 6-to-12-month minimum commitment before you can fairly evaluate them, and roughly 4-6% of men experience sexual side effects that mostly resolve after year one. They halve your PSA, which is a feature of the mechanism but a trap for anyone who forgets the doubling rule.

Neither drug treats prostatitis, CPPS, or cancer. Neither replaces diagnostic work. Neither should be started without knowing your prostate volume and PSA baseline.

But for a man with a genuinely enlarged prostate and a real risk of retention or surgery, these are the most consequential medications in the BPH toolkit. Judged on the right timeline, they deliver.
