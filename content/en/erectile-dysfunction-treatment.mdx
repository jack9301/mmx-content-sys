There's a particular kind of quiet resignation that attaches to erectile dysfunction. Men tend to treat it as a fact of aging rather than a problem to be solved — the sense that the body has simply reached the end of that particular capability, and the sensible response is to make peace with it.

That framing is wrong. ED is one of the most treatable conditions in medicine. The reason so many men never get treated is that they assume it's untreatable, and the reason so many who do get treated quit early is that the first thing they try doesn't work and they conclude the whole thing doesn't work.

Both of those are fixable. But fixing them requires understanding the treatment ladder properly, including the parts that aren't glamorous.

## Before treatment: three questions that change everything

A good clinician will sort out these three things before reaching for a prescription, and if yours doesn't, it's worth asking.

**Is this vascular or psychogenic?** This is the foundational distinction, and the two have completely different treatment pathways.

**Organic ED** — from vascular, neurological, hormonal or structural causes — has a characteristic signature: **gradual onset and progressive worsening**, usually with **harder erections in the morning** and a general decline in quality rather than intermittent failure. The Princeton Consensus identifies endothelial dysfunction as the most common underlying mechanism.

**Psychogenic ED** typically comes on **abruptly**, tends to be **situational** — fine in one context, not another — and often coexists with **morning erections that remain normal**. The absence of that physical basis is what distinguishes it.

This distinction isn't academic. If the driver is psychogenic, a PDE5 inhibitor on its own often makes things *worse* — because it introduces an expectation that then has to be met, which increases the performance anxiety that caused the problem in the first place.

**Is it situational or global?** ED that only appears in one specific context, with a new partner, or in a particular setting, is a different problem from ED that happens everywhere. Situational ED often needs no medication at all.

**Is something else underneath it?** Diabetes, hypertension, hypogonadism, depression, medication side effects. A man with ED and untreated hypertension has a cardiovascular problem that happens to also affect his erections.

## The cardiovascular conversation

This is the part worth having even if you never intend to take a medication.

ED and cardiovascular disease share a root cause: **endothelial dysfunction**. The lining of blood vessels fails to produce enough nitric oxide, which is exactly the signalling pathway that erection depends on. The penile arteries are small, which means they show the damage before larger vessels do.

The epidemiology runs consistently in one direction. Large cohort studies and meta-analyses find elevated rates of cardiovascular events and mortality in men with ED compared with men without it. One meta-analysis of longitudinal studies found ED associated with roughly a **twofold increase** in cardiovascular events and cardiovascular mortality.

Let me be precise about what that does and doesn't mean, because it's easy to overstate:

- **Association is strong and consistent.** ED predicts cardiovascular risk.
- **Causation is not established.** ED does not cause heart disease; both reflect a shared underlying process.
- **Practical implication:** ED in a younger man with no other risk factors is a reason to get blood pressure, lipids, glucose and testosterone checked. Not a reason to panic.

![Cross-sections of blood vessels showing the endothelial lining, with healthy open lumen on one side and thickened narrowed lumen on the other](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1793864340_99037e4b.png)

The 2024 Princeton Consensus explicitly recommends that men with ED — particularly young men with ED — be evaluated for cardiovascular risk factors. That's a genuinely useful piece of advice that gets skipped in a lot of ED care.

## Lifestyle: what actually moves the needle

There's a temptation to present lifestyle as a soft alternative to treatment. It isn't — for some men it *is* treatment, and for most men it changes the underlying biology that the medication is compensating for.

**Smoking** is the most damaging modifiable factor. It causes endothelial dysfunction, and the effect is measurable: smokers have substantially higher rates of ED, and endothelial function measurably improves within months of stopping.

**Physical activity** has the strongest evidence base of any lifestyle intervention for erectile function, and it's not close. Regular aerobic exercise improves endothelial function, and men who exercise regularly have markedly lower rates of ED than sedentary peers. This is also the intervention most likely to help the underlying vascular cause rather than mask it.

**Obesity** matters both directly and indirectly — through endothelial dysfunction, through testosterone reduction, and through the sleep apnoea and cardiovascular risk that come with it.

**Alcohol** deserves precision, because the folk belief is backwards. Alcohol acutely impairs erectile function; the myth that it helps is really about the short-term reduction in anxiety. And the relationship is dose-dependent: heavy long-term drinking damages erectile function through liver, nerve and hormonal effects.

**Sleep** is under-rated and there's a specific mechanism: **poor sleep suppresses testosterone**. Men sleeping under five hours have testosterone levels measurably comparable to men ten years older. There's also a strong overlap between obstructive sleep apnoea and ED, and treating apnoea can improve erectile function in men who have both.

**Pornography and masturbation** get discussed constantly and are worth handling factually rather than morally. The relevant mechanisms are arousal patterns, vasocongestion and recovery time, and conditioning — specifically whether repeated high-intensity stimulation has trained arousal to prefer a specific pattern that doesn't transfer to partnered sex. There's also a well-described phenomenon of penile sensitivity changes and delayed ejaculation associated with heavy use. If ED appeared alongside a change in masturbation habits, that's worth raising with a clinician, because it's a modifiable factor and it can be modified.

## PDE5 inhibitors: the first-line medications

These are the drugs everyone means. Sildenafil (Viagra), tadalafil (Cialis), vardenafil, avanafil.

**How they work, briefly.** Erection is largely a vascular event mediated by nitric oxide and cyclic GMP. PDE5 inhibitors block the enzyme that breaks down cyclic GMP, so the signal persists. They don't create an erection — they let the body's own response work properly, which is why they need stimulation and why they don't fix problems with desire, arousal or the nerves themselves.

**The four drugs differ mainly in speed and duration:**

| Drug | Onset | Duration | Notable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sildenafil** | 30–60 min | ~4 hours | Most studied; food matters a lot |
| **Tadalafil** | up to 35 min | up to 36 hours | Spontaneity; also the LUTS drug |
| **Vardenafil** | 30–60 min | ~4–5 hours | Similar profile to sildenafil |
| **Avanafil** | 15–30 min | ~4 hours | Fastest onset, fewer food interactions |

The tadalafil duration is worth dwelling on, because it matters for more than convenience. A 36-hour window means sex doesn't have to be scheduled around a pill. That removes a performance-anxiety driver that single-use, short-acting medication can itself create.

**Success rates.** In healthy men with no vascular disease, PDE5 inhibitors work for roughly **70–80%** of attempts. Rates are lower in men with diabetes, vascular disease, or after pelvic surgery, which is a genuine biological difference rather than a dosing failure.

### The single most important practical point

**PDE5 inhibitors do not work on an empty stomach.** High-fat meals delay absorption of sildenafil substantially and blunt the peak effect. This is the single most common reason a first trial "didn't work," and it's why so many men conclude the drug is useless for them.

There's a related point about timing: the first dose is often taken under poor conditions — stressed, in a hurry, in an unfamiliar setting. The drug needs time to work and it needs stimulation. That combination is a poor test.

### "It didn't work for me" — and why that's often fixable

One of the more striking findings in the ED literature came from an audit of men recorded as non-responders to PDE5 inhibitors. **Around 69% of them were using the drug incorrectly**, and after structured re-education — correct timing, correct dose escalation, correcting food and alcohol, and ensuring adequate stimulation — **around 76.5% recovered erectile function.**

That's a striking result. It means a large share of the men who believe they "don't respond to Viagra" do respond, once the error is identified.

![A man at a kitchen table with a pill bottle and a glass of water](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1793865152_3275a44e.png)

Common correctable errors:
- Taking it on a full stomach
- Taking it with a large amount of alcohol
- Not trying again at a higher dose — most guidelines allow escalation
- Expecting it to work without arousal or without adequate time
- Taking it at the wrong point in the session

### The dangerous interaction

**Nitrates and PDE5 inhibitors must never be combined.** Nitrates include nitroglycerin and nitrate-based angina medication. The combination can cause a catastrophic drop in blood pressure. This is not a theoretical caution — it's the reason PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated in men on nitrates, and it's why any clinician prescribing them asks about angina medication before prescribing.

Related: **alpha-blockers**, used for BPH, can interact with PDE5 inhibitors and drop blood pressure too. This matters if you're taking both — and it's exactly the scenario covered in [Alpha Blockers for BPH](https://www.agubi.com/en/articles/alpha-blockers-bph/). The two are not usually taken at the same time, and the timing spacing that clinicians use is a decision for the prescriber to make with both conditions in mind.

### The dropout problem

PDE5 inhibitors have high discontinuation rates, and the reasons are usually fixable: side effects, cost, inconvenient timing, wrong dose, or an expectation set in the wrong place.

The ORDEAL trial, published in *The Lancet* in 2024, looked at real-world effectiveness in men who had **already discontinued** oral PDE5 inhibitors. Among those who restarted treatment, a substantial majority regained satisfactory erections — with effectiveness depending substantially on **adherence to correct usage and on whether cardiovascular risk factors were present**.

That last finding is the key: men with cardiovascular risk factors did worse, which is consistent with ED in that group being more often genuinely vascular, and therefore less responsive to a drug that only amplifies existing function.

## Testosterone: what it does and doesn't do

Testosterone is the most over-prescribed and most misunderstood part of ED treatment.

**It works if you're genuinely deficient.** In men with **hypogonadism** — clinically low testosterone with the right symptoms — restoring levels improves erectile function, and this is a legitimate treatment.

**It works much less well if you're not.** The evidence for testosterone as a general ED treatment in men with **normal** levels is weak. The large Testosterone Trials found no consistent benefit for erectile function in men who weren't deficient, and flagged cardiovascular safety signals that remain debated.

**Getting tested properly matters.** Two early-morning fasting levels on separate days, not one. Levels fluctuate, and a single afternoon reading will mislead you. Total testosterone is the standard measure; free testosterone and SHBG can clarify borderline cases.

If you do take testosterone, it's monitored — blood counts, PSA, and sometimes a testosterone level check to confirm you're in a reasonable range rather than overshooting. Testosterone is also not an acute-treatment tool; the effect on erectile function takes weeks, and it doesn't replace PDE5 inhibitors for situational problems.

## Second-line options

The ladder exists, and most men never get past the first rung.

**Vacuum erection devices** are considerably underused. They create a reliable erection mechanically, work regardless of vascular or psychological cause, have no drug interactions, and are effective for a substantial proportion of men. The main complaint is discomfort and the manual effort, which is why they tend to be abandoned rather than not tried.

**Intraurethral alprostadil and intracavernosal injections** are the most effective non-surgical options available, and they work in men who respond poorly to oral medication — including men with vascular ED. They require training and carry real side-effect risks, including pain, prolonged erections requiring emergency treatment (priapism), and fibrosis with long-term use. They're underused because they require a clinic visit and a level of comfort most ED discussions don't reach.

**Penile implants** are the last step, for men who have failed everything else. The satisfaction rates are genuinely high — and the modern devices are considerably better than their reputation. This is a real and legitimate option that men often don't know exists, and delaying it costs years of unnecessary suffering.

## Psychological ED deserves specific treatment

If the driver is anxiety rather than blood vessels, medication alone often fails — and sometimes backfires.

The performance anxiety cycle is straightforward: anxiety → poor erection → heightened anxiety → worse erection. Each repetition strengthens the association.

What works:
- **Sex therapy or CBT**, specifically aimed at ED, which has real evidence and is under-prescribed
- **Reducing the performance stakes**, which the long-duration option (tadalafil) does better than short-acting medication
- **Treating the physical problems that fuel the anxiety** — pain, sleep, relationship tension, or genuine vascular disease
- **Not treating the first failed attempt as a verdict.** One failure under bad conditions is not a diagnosis of "it doesn't work for me"

If you're in a relationship, partner involvement tends to make a measurable difference — the problem is rarely the man's alone, and treating it as an individual failing makes it worse.

## What actually happens at the appointment

![A doctor consulting with a male patient across a desk](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1793865284_09d41ca4.png)

It should be unremarkable, and if it isn't, that's information.

A reasonable appointment includes: a discussion of onset, pattern and context; blood pressure; a general cardiovascular and metabolic review; and testosterone if symptoms and history suggest it. Blood tests for glucose or lipids if not done recently.

What should raise a flag:
- A prescription handed over with no questions about your history
- No mention of cardiovascular risk factors
- No discussion of whether your ED is situational or global
- Being told your ED is just age

And what you don't have to accept: a prescription isn't the same as a plan. If the first drug doesn't work, the right response is to work out why — which usually means checking usage, dose, timing and the underlying vascular and psychological factors, not moving on to something else.

## The ladder, honestly

**First line** — lifestyle and modifiable risk factors, plus a PDE5 inhibitor if wanted. Most men find a workable combination here.

**Second line** — vacuum device, topical or intraurethral medication, injections, and targeted psychological treatment. Genuinely underused.

**Third line** — penile implants. Highly effective, and the delay in reaching them is usually the patient's, not the clinician's.

**Throughout** — the cardiovascular conversation, because that's where the ED is pointing.

## The one thing to take away

The most common failure isn't that treatment doesn't work. It's that people try one thing, in the wrong conditions, and stop.

A man who takes a PDE5 inhibitor on a heavy stomach, once, at the wrong moment, and concludes he's finished with the whole subject has learned nothing about his own body except something false. And a man who assumes ED is an unavoidable stage of life has accepted a limitation that in most cases isn't one.
