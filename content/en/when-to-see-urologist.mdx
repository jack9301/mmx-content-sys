Most men know when something feels off in their body. The harder question is what to do about it. Prostate symptoms sit in an awkward middle ground — embarrassing enough to delay, vague enough to rationalize, sometimes serious enough that delay matters.

This article is a decision guide. It sorts symptoms into "go now," "book this week," and "mention it at your next checkup." It is not a substitute for medical advice, but it is a map of where your symptoms likely fall, what to expect at the appointment, and what to do if you cannot get an appointment quickly.

If at any point you are unsure, the right answer is to call a clinic and describe the symptom to a nurse. They will route you correctly.

## Go to the emergency room now

![Abstract emergency signs requiring immediate medical attention](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1791427846_f607f97f.png)

These combinations are not "wait and see." They suggest an acute process that can worsen quickly.

- **Fever with pelvic pain or painful urination.** This pattern is acute bacterial prostatitis until proven otherwise. The prostate is a small gland, but infection there can spread. It usually responds well to antibiotics when treated promptly — and gets much harder to treat when delayed.
- **Sudden inability to urinate at all.** The bladder fills, the lower abdomen becomes painfully distended, and the kidneys can be affected. Acute urinary retention is a medical emergency that needs catheterization. Do not try to "wait it out."
- **Visible blood in the urine with new-onset severe pain.** A stone that has started moving can become lodged in the urinary tract. The combination of gross hematuria and colicky flank or pelvic pain warrants same-day evaluation.
- **Painful scrotal swelling with fever.** The differential includes testicular torsion — a true emergency where the testicle loses blood supply within hours. Even if it turns out to be epididymitis or an abscess, those also need treatment.
- **A hard, irregular, painless lump in the testicle that has not been there before.** Testicular cancer is highly treatable when caught early. Any new persistent testicular mass warrants prompt ultrasound.

If you are reading this because one of these is happening right now, stop reading and go.

## Book an appointment this week

These symptoms are not emergencies, but they should not be ignored for months. The "watchful waiting" advice that applies to mild BPH does not apply here.

- **Blood in urine or semen that is new and persistent.** Even small amounts, recurring over a week or more, warrant a workup. Causes range from infection to stones to (less commonly) cancer, but you cannot tell which without imaging and labs.
- **Pain that wakes you from sleep or does not respond to over-the-counter pain relief.** Pelvic, perineal, or rectal pain that persists beyond a week, or recurs in short cycles, suggests inflammatory prostatitis or another condition that does not self-resolve.
- **Painful ejaculation that is new.** Several conditions cause this, including prostatitis, seminal vesiculitis, and post-ejaculatory pain syndrome. The good news: most are treatable once identified.
- **Urinary symptoms that are new and worsening over days to weeks.** A weak stream that gets weaker, urgency that gets sharper, nocturia that escalates from once to four times a night — these are not "just getting older." They are signs that something needs evaluation.
- **A burning sensation during urination that lasts more than two or three days.** A short-lived burning after sex or after holding urine is usually nothing. Burning that lingers or recurs is a urinary tract infection or prostate inflammation that needs treatment.
- **Unexplained weight loss combined with new urinary symptoms.** In men over 50, this pattern warrants prompt imaging to rule out prostate cancer, especially with elevated PSA.

When you call the clinic, describe the symptom and timeline concisely. "I have had visible blood in my urine for ten days, no pain, no fever" gets you a faster appointment than "I'm not feeling well."

## Mention at your next routine checkup

These are not urgent. They are not nothing either. They are the slow drift symptoms that men often ignore for years and then wish they had not.

- **Getting up once or twice a night to urinate.** Common with age, often benign, but worth a baseline assessment.
- **A weak stream or hesitancy that has been there for years.** BPH progresses slowly. A baseline IPSS score gives you something to compare against later.
- **Mild pelvic heaviness or awareness of the prostate.** Worth checking once, then ignoring if normal.
- **Mild erectile changes.** The conversation may be uncomfortable; it is also a useful cardiovascular and hormonal marker.

These are the symptoms where watchful waiting is appropriate, with a plan to escalate if they worsen or new symptoms develop.

## Age-based screening: when PSA comes up

![Age-based PSA screening considerations](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1791428246_e960becb.png)

Most men will, at some point, have a PSA conversation with their doctor. The honest summary of current guidance:

- **Age 50**: baseline discussion is reasonable for men at average risk.
- **Age 45**: start the discussion earlier if you have a family history of prostate cancer (father, brother) or are Black, both of which raise risk meaningfully.
- **Age 40 to 45**: an early baseline PSA can be useful for tracking trajectory later, but is not universally recommended.
- **Age 70+**: routine PSA screening is generally not recommended unless you have symptoms or a strong reason to suspect cancer. The harms of overdiagnosis in this age range usually outweigh the benefits.

PSA is a noisy test. An elevated result does not mean cancer — it can also mean prostatitis, BPH, recent ejaculation, or even a long bike ride. A normal result does not rule out cancer. PSA is one data point among several, and the trend over time matters more than any single number.

If your PSA comes back elevated, do not panic. The next step is usually a repeat test after a few weeks of avoiding ejaculation and prostate stimulation, often paired with imaging. Most men with elevated PSA do not have cancer. Most men who do have cancer have it caught early enough to treat effectively.

## The "embarrassing" symptoms men delay reporting

This is worth a section because it is the most common cause of late presentation. Men tend to delay reporting symptoms they associate with sexuality, masculinity, or weakness. The list, in order of how often it is delayed:

- **Erectile changes.** Many men wait two or more years before mentioning ED to a clinician. ED is also one of the earliest markers of cardiovascular disease. Mentioning it can save your life.
- **Pelvic pain during sex.** Often prostatitis. Highly treatable. Telling your doctor takes 30 seconds.
- **Discharge from the penis.** Usually an STI or prostatitis. Both are treatable. Delay makes them harder to manage and increases transmission risk.
- **Blood in semen.** Almost always benign, almost never discussed. Worth one mention to a doctor so they can decide if workup is needed.
- **A change in testicle size or shape.** Embarrassing, scary, easy to ignore. A two-minute exam and a five-minute ultrasound settle the question.

None of these are fun to bring up. All of them are easier to address when caught early.

## The two checklists worth filling out before your visit

![Symptom scales help you describe severity](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1791428054_b5fc98b0.png)

Bringing a completed symptom score to your appointment gives them something to work with besides memory.

**NIH-CPSI** (Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index) — for pain, urinary, and quality-of-life symptoms. A score below 8 is mild, 15–29 moderate, 30+ severe. A change of 6 points between visits is considered a real change.

**IPSS** (International Prostate Symptom Score) — for BPH-type urinary symptoms (frequency, urgency, weak stream, nocturia). 0–7 mild, 8–19 moderate, 20–35 severe.

You can find both as one-page questionnaires online. Filling one out takes five minutes and changes the quality of the appointment.

## How to prepare for the appointment

![Preparing for a urology appointment](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1791428482_a663abdf.png)

The appointment will go faster and be more useful if you arrive with:

- **A two-sentence description of your main symptom.** "I have had pelvic pain for six weeks, worse when sitting, no fever." That sentence steers the whole visit.
- **A timeline.** When did it start? Has it been constant or coming in episodes? What makes it better or worse?
- **A list of what you have already tried.** Medications, supplements, position changes, heat, anything else.
- **Your completed symptom score.**
- **A list of all medications and supplements.** Including over-the-counter.
- **A short family history.** Any prostate cancer in your father, brother, or uncles? Age at diagnosis?

The urologist may want to do a digital rectal exam. This is a 30-second, mildly awkward exam that provides real information about prostate size, shape, and consistency. It is not painful. The anxiety around it is worse than the exam.

## When you cannot get an appointment quickly

Sometimes the symptom is in the "book this week" category and the next available appointment is in four weeks. Options:

- **Call and explain urgency.** Front-desk staff triage every day. Be specific about the symptom and timeline.
- **Ask to be seen by another clinician in the same practice.** Wait times differ.
- **Visit an urgent care clinic.** They cannot do everything a urologist can, but they can rule out infection, do basic imaging, and start a workup that buys time.
- **Use a virtual visit for initial triage.** Many urology practices now offer this for symptom-based referrals.

If the symptom escalates — pain gets worse, fever appears, you cannot urinate — go to the emergency room. Do not wait for the appointment.

## What to do with this article

![Take action on prostate symptoms](https://cdn.hailuoai.com/mcp/image_tool/output/513289654133362689/410025998700867/1791428598_c251125c.png)

You came to this page for a reason. Pick the right bucket and act on it:

- If a symptom is in the "go now" list and is happening to you, put down the phone or step away from the screen and go.
- If a symptom is in the "this week" list, book the appointment in the next 24 hours. Do not "think about it." Do not "see if it gets better." Book.
- If a symptom is in the "mention at checkup" list, write it down somewhere you will find it before your next visit. Three months from now, you will not remember the symptom unless it is written down.

This article is informational and is not a substitute for medical advice. If anything above sounds familiar, please see a qualified clinician.
